As for the confusion that some may encounter due to statements like: "With these conditions we mentioned, it is permissible to follow interests," it should not be used as a pretext to claim that he advocates for istislah. Following interests is a natural inclination in humans, as people inherently pursue their interests. However, the subject of discussion is the evidence for the action that is intentionally performed for the sake of interest; is the evidence the interest itself? The assertion of interest is a consideration of the legality of the action based on it being an interest, and this is what al-Ghazali refutes. However, if the Sharia indicates the permissibility of the action, then there is nothing preventing its performance with the intention of serving the interest. The indication of the Sharia comes from the legal proofs and not from the fact that the interest is an interest. This, then, is the meaning of his statement "it is permissible to follow interests." And his statement "istislah is not a fifth principle in itself," and his statement: "rather, whoever seeks to establish it has legislated." Therefore, istislah is not considered valid according to al-Ghazali. Likewise, al-Amidi refuted istislah in al-Ihkam, saying(155): "The interests are divided in terms of the testimony of the Sharia into those that are recognized, those that are nullified, and those that the Sharia does not testify to either way. The latter is referred to as the unqualified suitable." He also said(156) - regarding the unqualified suitable -: "This category is uncertain between the two categories, and it is not more appropriate to associate it with one than the other, thus it is not permissible to argue with it without a recognized witness that identifies it as being among the recognized rather than the nullified."
Imam Izz al-Din ibn Abd al-Salam also rejected it. He said(157): "And likewise, there is no ruling except for Him (i.e., Allah), so His rulings are derived from the Book, the Sunnah, consensus, sound analogies, and recognized proofs. No one has the right to deem something good or to employ an unqualified interest, nor to imitate someone whom he has not been commanded to imitate."
Ibn Qudamah al-Maqdisi also refutes unqualified interests. He said(158): "... this is called an unqualified interest and we do not call it analogy, because analogy refers to a specific principle, and the correct view is that this is not a proof because it is not known.
