Some add three other sources to the seven mentioned above: 8- the consensus of the people of Medina, 9- taking the least that has been said, 10- inductive reasoning (istiqra). Al-Qarafi mentions other sources in addition to these ten.
Imam al-Nabhani (may Allah have mercy on him) says(44): "As for the proofs that are thought to be evidence but are not, they are those for which there is evidence indicating that they are a proof but are either speculative evidence or not applicable to what they are being used to argue, and the most important of them are four: the law of those before us, the school of thought of the Companion, juristic preference, and public interest."
He says about presumption of continuity(45): "Presumption of continuity is not a legal proof; otherwise, it would require a definitive proof for its establishment, and no definitive proof has been established for it. Rather, it is a legal ruling, and speculative evidence is sufficient for it."
It is clear from the definition of presumption of continuity – which is the affirmation of a matter in the second time based on its affirmation in the first time – that it is a method of understanding and reasoning, not a proof. It is necessitated by reason and sound judgment. Al-Amidi does not consider presumption of continuity an additional source beyond the four, nor does he categorize it among the illusory proofs in his view; rather, he associates it with legal rulings that are taken based on speculation. He says(46): "... what is confirmed to exist or not in a certain state necessitates a presumption of its continuity, and presumption is a valid proof in legal matters."
As for blocking the means, al-Amidi does not mention it among the accepted proofs nor among the illusory proofs in his view. Blocking the means is not a principle; rather, it is a rule that falls under a general principle, which is the principle of the consequences of actions.
