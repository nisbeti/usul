"With two palm fronds, about forty"(233). In a narration by al-Nasa'i, "the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) struck him with a sandal about forty times," and in a narration by Ahmad and al-Bayhaqi: "He commanded about twenty men, and each one struck him twice with the palm fronds and sandals." As for what has been narrated from Ali that he said: "The Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) struck forty, and Abu Bakr struck forty, and Umar struck eighty, and each year"(234), it indicates that the punishment was carried out by the Messenger (peace and blessings be upon him) forty times, and the Companions carried it out forty and eighty times. This means that the minimum is forty and the maximum is eighty, which is left to the discretion of the imam. Therefore, it is not among the public interests (maslaha mursala). He did not legislate eighty for the sake of a benefit, but it was left to the caliph to impose forty or eighty. As for what Umar did, it is a matter of managing affairs, which is what the caliph is allowed to do according to his opinion and ijtihad. Moreover, is not the punishment of eighty indicative of the command of the Messenger (peace and blessings be upon him) to strike forty with two palm fronds? And likewise, when the Companions strike eighty, and none among them denies that, is this not a consensus among them? Thus, there is no room here to speak of public interests (maslaha mursala) at all(235).

Z- Umar ibn al-Khattab sharing with the governors whom he suspects regarding their wealth due to the mixing of their private funds with the funds they have benefited from the authority of governance(236).

This action does not indicate any inference based on public interest. For the Sharia has granted the caliph the authority to manage the affairs of the Muslims according to his opinion and ijtihad. He appoints governors, holds them accountable, dismisses them, spends from the public treasury based on his opinion and ijtihad, concludes treaties and breaks them, directs armies for jihad, and other matters that the Sharia has assigned to him. His actions as a caliph stem from the rights granted to him by the Sharia. If he specifies a benefit he desires, the actions he undertakes to achieve it are not permissible solely for that benefit; rather, they must be indicated by the Sharia.
