The evidence indicating prohibition exists, but it is the inability to derive it that is present. One cannot rule that an action is not forbidden unless it is known—by evidence—that it is obligatory, recommended, permissible, or disliked. An action for which there is no indication of permissibility, but its permissibility is inferred from the absence of anything that prohibits it, what prevents one from saying: we did not find anything indicating its permissibility, and therefore it is forbidden? It should be noted that the absence is relative to the researcher and not to the Sharia, for the Sharia has a ruling for every action.

If it is said: your statement is opposed by the hadith of the Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him): "Indeed, Allah has prescribed obligations, so do not neglect them, and He has prohibited things, so do not violate them, and He has set limits, so do not transgress them. And He has pardoned certain matters out of mercy for you, not forgetting, so do not seek them out"(307), as well as what Ibn Majah and al-Tirmidhi narrated from Salman al-Farsi, who said: the Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) was asked about butter, cheese, and fur, and he said: "The lawful is what Allah has made lawful in His Book, and the unlawful is what Allah has made unlawful in His Book, and what He has left silent about is what He has pardoned for you." These texts indicate that we have been pardoned for what we do not find evidence for, and consequently, there is no blame or punishment, and that there are matters about which the legislator has remained silent. Accordingly, it is valid to infer from the absence of contradiction with the Qur'an, the Sunnah, consensus, and analogy regarding matters that fall within the realm of pardon, which are occurrences that have no ruling. I say: these texts should not be understood in a way that contradicts other texts, and the claim that there are matters or occurrences that have no ruling contradicts the definitive text: "And We have sent down to you the Book as a clarification for all things"(308), which implies that the Sharia is incomplete, and it contradicts the definitive text: "This day I have perfected for you your religion"(309). These are definitive texts, while those are reports of singular transmission, and in cases of contradiction, the definitive is given precedence. Thus, the meanings of the two hadiths and similar ones must be understood in a manner that does not lead to this conclusion. Therefore, the Sharia encompasses rulings for all occurrences, and silence does not mean the absence of a ruling or the absence of clarification. Rather, it is silence regarding prohibition specifically for what has been permitted by the general evidence of permissibility.
