It has already been established that there is no distinction between acts of worship (ibadat) and other matters referred to as customary practices (adat); all are legal rulings (ahkam shar'iyya), all are addresses from the Legislator (al-Shari'), and all are directed towards the accountable person (mukallaf). Acts of worship are a pure relationship between the servant and his Lord, which must be performed according to what Allah has commanded, while customary practices are a relationship between human beings that must also proceed according to the commands of Allah, Exalted is He(199).
This statement, which refers to paying attention to the meanings in customary practices, does not align with the true meaning intended by its proponents. They intended that the rulings of actions outside of acts of worship should be justified. The true meaning is that these actions are sought for their meaning, which is incorrect. Theft, adultery, prayer, and enjoining good and forbidding evil are actions for which meanings are not sought; rather, rulings are requested for them. Similarly, if we say that meanings are the causes (illal) – which is the aim of the proponents of this statement – this is also incorrect, as actions do not have a cause sought for them; the cause in Sharia is the cause of the ruling related to the action, not the cause of the action itself. For instance, distraction from prayer is the cause of the ruling, which is the prohibition of selling at the call to prayer on Friday; this distraction is not the cause of the sale or the prohibition of selling. As for what they intended, which is that the rulings of actions outside of acts of worship should be justified, these rulings are understood from texts or from what is suitable for inference. Some of these rulings come with justifications, whether the cause is taken explicitly, implicitly, through deduction, or analogy, while others come without justification. What is not justified cannot be justified by anyone after the Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him). Justification – in every case – leads to analogy (qiyas) and not to the claim of public interest (maslaha mursala). If it is said that meanings are the interests or the types of interests present in the rulings, this refers back to their original evidence, which we have refuted. In summary, the response here is that interests are not the meaning of the ruling nor of the text indicating the ruling. As for the action for which a ruling is sought, there is no meaning to be considered in legislating the ruling other than understanding its reality, and thereafter the ruling related to it is given from the texts, not from its reality, as its reality does not indicate anything.
