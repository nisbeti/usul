It is not related to public interest (maslaha) and harm (mafsada), for when Allah, Exalted is He, says: “Then a pledge is to be held in hand”(196) and when He says: “When you contract a debt for a fixed term, write it down”(197) and when He says: “And have witnesses when you trade”(198), He is merely clarifying the ruling on pledges, the ruling on writing down debts, and the ruling on witnessing in sales, without indicating whether this is a public interest or not, neither explicitly nor implicitly. The text does not imply that this ruling is a public interest or not, neither closely nor remotely, nor in any way whatsoever.

The second evidence: The approval of the Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) of Muadh when he sent him to Yemen to exercise his opinion if he did not find a ruling in the Book of Allah or in the Sunnah of His Messenger.

This is an argument in an inappropriate context. The hadith does not mention public interest, but the hadith – if authentic – is used as evidence for the permissibility of ijtihad (independent reasoning). The hadith does not state that the Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) approved Muadh's ijtihad based on public interest, neither in the explicit wording nor in the implied meaning. Rather, it is an approval for Muadh to exercise his opinion to judge according to what this ijtihad leads him to if he does not find the ruling in the Book or in the Sunnah. Muadh did not say that he would judge by his opinion; rather, he said: “I will exercise my opinion.” This means that if he does not find the ruling explicitly in the Book or in the Sunnah, he will exert his effort to derive it. Even if he had said: “I will judge by my opinion,” there is nothing in the hadith that indicates that his ruling by his opinion would be based on public interest. From where does the understanding come that the Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) approved istislah (public interest) or masalih mursala (unrestricted public interests) through this hadith? This is an assertion without evidence.

The third evidence: Their statement that the origin in customs is to pay attention to meanings and the motives of legislation, which is contrary to acts of worship that are restricted to the limits of the text.
