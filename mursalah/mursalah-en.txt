Introduction
The study of the authority of public interest (maslaha mursala) is a foundational issue that has been debated both in the past and in modern times. However, there is a clear distinction between the disagreements among the imams and usulists in the early periods, which were characterized by a multitude of scholars, the flourishing of knowledge and jurisprudence, and the dominance of Islam, and the disagreements among contemporary scholars in the later periods, which are marked by a scarcity of scholars and their reliance on holding certificates.
In the early periods, the majority of scholars held that public interest is not a valid proof. In contrast, in the later periods—such as our own—you can hardly find anyone who engages in a proper legal discussion on this matter, nor can you find anyone who rejects it.
Moreover, in these recent times—characterized by the decline of Islam and the subjugation of Muslims to their enemies, as well as the replacement of Islamic concepts with Western or Eastern concepts derived from disbelief—you find scholars who are agents of disbelief and its rulers, who have taken prominent positions in knowledge and fatwa, altering the rulings of Allah and issuing fatwas declaring the unlawful as lawful, under the pretext of public interest and the changing times, and under the pretext that the objectives of the Sharia (maqasid al-sharia) must be preserved, regardless of how this preservation is achieved. Thus, you see usury becoming permissible, the rulings on punishments changing, and there being no issue with this as long as the goal is deterrence and prevention; we can deter in ways other than the old methods. Mixing between genders becomes widespread, seeking assistance from disbelievers to fight Muslims becomes a lawful act, and making peace with Israel and recognizing its sovereignty over Muslim lands becomes something encouraged by Islam, as it promotes peace.
For this reason, many of the certainties and fundamentals of Islam are obscured, and many foreign ideas are promoted as if they are legal principles, when they are not. Such as: "what has become widespread is accepted," and "the change of rulings with the change of times cannot be denied," and "wherever there is public interest, there is the law of Allah," and customary practices, among others. The truth is that what is unlawful remains unlawful even when it becomes widespread, and the rulings cannot be denied to change with the change of time, as...


[Page 8]

Revelation has ceased, and God's ruling on any given incident is established and can only be lifted by evidence from the Sharia. The Sharia of God is not where the public interest (maslaha) lies; if we wish, we can say: the public interest is where the Sharia is. Custom ('urf) has no value unless the Sharia indicates something from it; then it is taken as a proof of the Sharia and not because it is customary.
Due to this deviation in understanding Islam and the deliberate misguidance—often invoked under the pretext of public interest—I have undertaken a study of the topic of public interests in a legal and foundational manner, hoping that it may have an impact in preventing deviation and in raising the awareness of Muslims about the true nature of their religion.
I have divided the book into four chapters and a short conclusion:
The first chapter: On the science of usul al-fiqh (principles of jurisprudence) in terms of its definition and subject matter, and on the ruler and his uniqueness in determining good and evil, then on the sources of legislation, both agreed upon and disputed.
The second chapter: On the objectives of the Sharia (maqasid al-sharia) and clarifying that they are the goals of legislation and not reasons, the difference between the objectives of the Sharia and the objectives of rulings, and in bringing about benefits and averting harms, then on reasoning by analogy and the categories of analogy and the categories of public interests.
The third chapter: On public interests in terms of their definition, the evidence of those who advocate for them, and the scholars' positions on them, then a foundational discussion of them, and a jurisprudential discussion of examples from the actions of the Companions (may Allah be pleased with them). The discussion included evidence that public interests are not a legal proof and that the actions of the Companions are not considered public interests.
The fourth chapter: It includes an investigation into the intentions of the imams and scholars who spoke of public interests. It also includes a response to two doubts regarding the classification of interests into valid, invalid, and neither valid nor invalid, and regarding placing conditions on them that they do not contradict a text, consensus, or analogy.
The conclusion: On acting with the intention of public interest, and on the respected legal opinion even if it contradicts another ijtihad, and on the opinion that is rejected legally even if it is claimed to be derived from correct ijtihad.

Principles of Jurisprudence

"Principles of Jurisprudence" is a compound term whose understanding depends on knowing its two components. The term "origin" in language refers to what is built upon, whether the building is physical, like the walls built on a foundation, or mental, like the effect (ma'lul) built upon its cause (illah) and the sign (dليل) built upon its indication. Thus, the origin is the rule, and the principles of jurisprudence (usul al-fiqh) are the rules upon which jurisprudence is built.

Jurisprudence (fiqh) in terminology is defined as: the knowledge of practical legal rulings derived from their detailed evidence. In contrast to the principles, these rulings are called subsidiary rulings (ahkam far'iyya). Jurisprudence is also used in terminology to refer to the legal rulings themselves, so we say: this is a book of jurisprudence, meaning it contains legal rulings.

As for the legal ruling, it is the address of the legislator (shari') concerning the actions of the servants - and it is said: the obligated (mukallaf) - by way of command, choice, or status. This excludes the rulings of beliefs because their subject is faith and not actions. Our statement: derived from their detailed evidence means that every ruling has its specific evidence. For example, His saying, Exalted is He: {And pilgrimage to the House is a duty upon the people} is a detailed evidence for a legal ruling, which is the obligation of pilgrimage, and His Messenger (peace and blessings be upon him) said: "I had prohibited you from visiting graves, but now visit them"(1) is a detailed evidence for a specific legal ruling.

Therefore, the principles of jurisprudence are: the rules upon which the knowledge of practical legal rulings derived from their detailed evidence is built, meaning the rules that are used to derive these rulings from their evidence. Its subject is the rulings and the evidence. That is, the definition of the ruling and its types, and knowing the ruler or legislator, meaning the one who has the authority to issue the ruling, and the sources of the ruling such as the Qur'an and the Sunnah and what they guide to from other sources, and the subject of the ruling, meaning the obligated person to implement the ruling. The ruler

He is the one to whom the issuance of the ruling is referred, that is, the one in authority to command and prohibit. The ruling being discussed here is the legal ruling (hukm shar'i) upon which reward and punishment are based. It is not the ruling that a person finds pleasing either through his intellect or his desires, nor is it the ruling that is presumed to bring about benefit as perceived by a person. It is well known that the intellect is incapable of fully grasping the needs of humanity and is even more incapable of providing solutions for them. It is also known that a person's intellect may perceive something good as evil, and something evil as good. A person may find pleasing what others find repugnant, and his estimation of benefit lacks complete understanding of the realities of all people's issues, as well as knowledge of the outcomes and consequences of people's actions and behaviors. Allah, Exalted is He, said: “Fighting has been enjoined upon you while it is hateful to you. And perhaps you hate a thing and it is good for you, and perhaps you love a thing and it is bad for you. And Allah knows, while you do not know” {2:216}(3). The story of our master Moses (peace be upon him) with al-Khidr (peace be upon him) is clear in this regard, as our master Moses (peace be upon him) disapproved of and found repugnant the actions of al-Khidr, which were carried out by revelation from the Lawgiver(4).

It is true that minds may agree on finding some matters pleasing and others repugnant, such as finding truthfulness pleasing and falsehood repugnant, and finding justice pleasing and oppression repugnant. However, this approval or disapproval stems from an innate disposition in humans - that is how the Creator has fashioned them - they are inclined towards certain matters and repelled by others. Yet, this inclination or repulsion has no bearing on determining reward and punishment; it affects a person's stance as a human being - that is, with all the characteristics of humanity, including incapacity, deficiency, need, love, hatred, preference, and aversion, satisfaction and anger, knowledge and ignorance, intelligence and foolishness, forgetfulness and confusion, and so forth - and does not lead to reward or punishment. Rather, reward and punishment depend on obedience or disobedience to the one who rewards and punishes. Therefore, the approval or disapproval of the intellect regarding something or an action does not determine the reward or punishment.

There is a matter concerning the entitlement to reward or punishment, and it does not define what is good and what is bad in the Sharia. The ruling that determines entitlement to reward or punishment is solely the ruling of Allah, so the ruler is only Allah. What is good is what Allah has commanded, and what is bad is what Allah has prohibited.
Therefore, we see that lying, which is naturally repugnant, can sometimes be good, as in war, or towards a wife, or for reconciliation. Similarly, injustice, as what may be perceived by reason as injustice could be justice, and vice versa, because reason in its judgments and taste in what it finds acceptable are greatly influenced by what they are accustomed to in their environment.
The Sharia has also indicated that the ruler is Allah, Exalted is He, alone. Allah, the Exalted, said: “Indeed, the judgment is only for Allah”(5). And He said: “And it is not for a believing man or a believing woman, when Allah and His Messenger have decreed a matter, that they should have any choice in their matter”(6), and He said: “Indeed, to Him belongs creation and command”(7).
Thus, the ruler is Allah, Exalted is He, alone. And if there is a disagreement among the theologians regarding the matter of good and bad by reason, there is no disagreement among them that sovereignty belongs to Allah alone.
What we have presented indicates that there is no ruling before the arrival of the Sharia, for before the arrival of the Sharia, there is no means to know the ruling of Allah. What reason may arrive at in terms of rulings, and what people may agree upon in terms of laws and customs, does not lead to reward or punishment, as obedience to them is not obedience to Allah, and disobedience to them is not disobedience to Allah; they are not rulings from Allah, Exalted is He. Al-Shatibi said(8): “The original ruling regarding actions before the arrival of the Sharia is the nullification of obligation, for there is no ruling upon him before knowing the ruling, as the condition for obligation according to the usul scholars is knowledge of what one is obligated to.”

Sources of Legislation

Introduction
Since Allah, Exalted is He, is the sole legislator, it is necessary for humans to know His legislation to understand what to believe in, how to worship Allah, how to fulfill their needs, address their problems, and establish their lives with others. Hence, throughout the ages, people have needed messengers to convey to them the laws of Allah, Exalted is He.
Muhammad (peace and blessings be upon him) was the last of the prophets, and his message was the final message. After delivering the message, he (peace and blessings be upon him) passed away and left us with the Qur'an and the Sunnah. He said: "... adhere to my Sunnah and the Sunnah of the rightly guided caliphs; cling to it tightly, and beware of newly invented matters..."(9). And Allah, Exalted is He, said: "And whatever you disagree about, its ruling is with Allah"(10). He also said: "O you who have believed, obey Allah and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you. And if you disagree over anything, refer it to Allah and the Messenger if you should believe in Allah and the Last Day. That is the best [way] and best in result"(11).
Thus, this message serves as legislation for all people until the Day of Judgment, containing rulings for every incident. Since human life is filled with ongoing incidents, events, issues, and problems, it is always necessary to have someone who knows Allah's ruling on every new matter, which necessitates the knowledge of the sources from which rulings are derived. These are legal rulings that were all revealed to the Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) through revelation, "And he does not speak from [his own] inclination. It is not but a revelation revealed"(12). Therefore, the sources from which rulings are derived, that is, the sources of legislation or the principles of legislation, are those that are definitively proven(13) to be revelation from Allah.

The sources of legislation must be definitive.
The sources of legislation in Islam are the avenues in which Allah, Exalted is He, has deposited His commands and prohibitions. Therefore, researching the sources is one of the most important studies in the principles of jurisprudence (usul al-fiqh) after the study of the ruler, and the two topics are directly related.
The sources of legislation are among the fundamental principles, thus they are linked to beliefs in terms of being universal, and in terms of the necessity for them to be definitive. Just as beliefs cannot be derived from conjecture, and it is not permissible to rely on conjecture in acquiring them due to His saying, {And most of them follow not except assumption. Indeed, assumption avails not against the truth at all}(14) and other verses, likewise the principles of jurisprudence cannot be derived from conjecture, and nothing that is conjectural is considered among the principles.
If it were permissible to accept a source for legislation based on conjecture, this would mean that it is permissible to regard conjecture or predominant conjecture from any universal source as revelation. An example of this would be to say that the Gospel and the Torah are sources of legislation, because although they are distorted according to the text of the Qur'an, it is rationally and legally permissible that some of their texts are not distorted, and these texts could potentially be used as evidence for rulings by those who believe that the legislation of those before us is legislation for us, because some texts may be conjectured to be unaltered, just as the Sunnah is a source for rulings despite the existence of weak or fabricated hadiths within it. However, this is false, as it has been narrated that he (peace and blessings be upon him) saw a piece of the Torah with Umar ibn al-Khattab, and he became angry and said: "Did I not bring it to you white and pure? If my brother Moses were to come to me, he would have no choice but to follow me"(15).

And also, if we were to rely solely on conjecture in the principles, it would be necessary to consider seeing the Messenger (peace and blessings be upon him) in a dream as a source, due to his saying: "Whoever sees me in a dream, it is as if he has seen me while awake"(16), and other statements that convey the same meaning. However, no one whose opinion is respected has made such a claim(17).

Therefore, the principles, like the beliefs, must be certain. Just as beliefs are only taken from certainty, so too are the principles only considered with certainty, and conjecture is only accepted in the branches. Thus, the report of the singular (khabar al-ahad) is a proof in rulings but not in beliefs.

Al-Shatibi(18) said: "The principles of jurisprudence in religion are definitive, not conjectural, and the evidence for this is that they pertain to the universals of the Sharia, and whatever is thus is definitive." He also said(19): "If conjecture were permissible as a basis in the principles of jurisprudence, it would also be permissible as a basis in the principles of religion, which is not the case by consensus... because the relationship of the principles of jurisprudence to the foundation of the Sharia is like that of the principles of religion, and although they differ in rank, they are equal in that they are considered universals in every community." Al-Shatibi adds(20): "Some have said: There is no way to establish the principles of the Sharia through conjecture, because it is legislation, and we have only been commanded by conjecture in the branches." Imam Jamal al-Din Abdul Rahim al-Asnawi states in his book "Nihayat al-Sul" when discussing the indication of (do) in terms of the fact that conjectural evidence is not considered in the principles: "As for singular reports, they are invalid because the narration of singular reports, if it is beneficial, only provides conjecture, and the legislator has only permitted conjecture in practical matters, which are the branches, not in scientific matters such as the principles of religion and likewise the principles of jurisprudence, as conveyed by al-Anbari, the commentator on al-Burhan, from all the scholars."

Based on the above, the principles of jurisprudence, including sources of legislation, must be definitive.

The usuliyyin (jurisprudential scholars) agreed on some sources of legislation, while they differed on others. Among what they agreed upon are: the Qur'an, the Sunnah, consensus (ijma), and analogy (qiyas), and this agreement is what the majority holds. The Qur'an alone is something that there is no disagreement about at all; however, the other three are associated with wide-ranging and impactful disagreements in deriving rulings. The definition of the Sunnah differs between the Twelvers and the Ahl al-Sunnah, and there is also disagreement regarding consensus: whether it is the consensus of the mujtahidin (jurists) in a particular era, or the consensus of the Companions, or the consensus of the people of Medina, or the consensus of the Ahl al-Bayt, or the consensus of the Ummah. As for analogy, there is disagreement regarding it and in determining the rationale (illah) and its pathways.

The first source: the Book
"The Book is the Qur'an revealed to our master Muhammad (peace and blessings be upon him). It is what has been transmitted to us between the covers of the mushaf in the seven letters through a continuous chain of narration"(21).
This is a definitive source, and there is no disagreement among Muslims about it. It has been established in creed that the Qur'an is the speech of Allah, and it is definitive and certain. Therefore, it is a source of legislation. As the usuliyyin say, it is the first evidence.

The second source: the Sunnah
In language, the Sunnah means the way, and in Sharia, it refers to the acts of worship that are voluntary and transmitted from the Prophet (peace be upon him). It may also refer to what the Messenger said, did, or approved. When discussing legal evidence, the Sunnah refers to the actions of the Messenger, his sayings, and his approvals; all of this is the Sunnah(22).
The Sunnah is a source of legislation, and it is the second evidence after the Book. It is exactly like the Book in terms of its authority, as Allah, Exalted is He, said: "Say, 'I only warn you by revelation'"(23). And He said: "And whatever the Messenger has given you, take it; and what he has forbidden you, refrain from it."

The third source: Consensus
The scholars have differed regarding consensus: Consensus of whom? Some say it is the consensus of the Ummah or the consensus of the scholars, while others say it is the consensus of the mujtahids in a particular era, and some say it is the consensus of the people of Medina. The Shia have attributed to their belief the consensus of the Ahl al-Bayt or the consensus of the progeny, and it has been said that it is the consensus of the Companions. There is discussion regarding the possibility of consensus occurring after the Companions, and it has been attributed to Imam Ahmad (may Allah be pleased with him): "Whoever claims consensus is a liar."

Consensus is the consensus of the Companions
Whoever examines the evidence will find that the decisive proofs indicate the consensus of the Companions, while the evidence for others is speculative. Therefore, there is a distinction between definitive consensus and speculative consensus, and for this reason, those who deny the authority of the consensus of the Companions are deemed disbelievers, while those who deny the authority of consensus in general are not deemed disbelievers. It has been established in the principles of jurisprudence (usul al-fiqh) that the evidence must be definitive, and the evidence for the consensus of the Companions is definitive. Allah, Exalted is He, said: “And the foremost (in faith) among the Muhajirin (emigrants) and the Ansar (helpers) and those who followed them in goodness—Allah is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him. And He has prepared for them gardens beneath which rivers flow, wherein they will abide eternally. That is the great success”(33). And He said: “For the poor emigrants who were expelled from their homes and their properties, seeking bounty from Allah and His pleasure and supporting Allah and His Messenger. It is they who are the truthful. And those who were settled in the abode and (in faith) before them love those who emigrated to them and find not in their breasts any need of what they were given, but give preference over themselves, even if they were in privation. And whoever is protected from the stinginess of his soul—it is they who will be the successful”(34). These are definitive proofs in their establishment and indication, as they praise the Companions in general, not as individuals. Thus, Allah's statement: “Allah is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him,” and His statement: “It is they who are the truthful,” and His statement: “It is they who will be the successful” are all definitive statements indicating the truthfulness of those praised.
Then the Companions, both the Emigrants (Muhajirin) and the Helpers (Ansar), are the ones who transmitted the Qur'an to us, and if the possibility of error in the Sharia were to affect their consensus, it would have been possible for error to affect the Qur'an, which is impossible. Therefore, it is necessary to affirm the impossibility of their consensus being based on error in religious matters.
The other consensus (ijma) are not proofs because there is no definitive evidence for them. Likewise, it is not accepted that they indicate what they have been used to support. Thus, every consensus that is not that of the Companions (sahaba) is not a legal proof, because there is no definitive evidence that it is a legal proof, and all that they have used as evidence are speculative proofs, and because the proofs

The fourth source: analogy (qiyas)
It is also one of the evidences agreed upon by the majority, but it is a subject of extensive acceptance and rejection and debate among its proponents, as is evident from its various definitions.
Analogy in language means estimation, and among the jurists, its definitions have varied(35), and the most accurate definition is: establishing a similar ruling for a known case in another known case due to their shared cause of the ruling according to the one establishing it(36). Its components are four: the branch that is to be measured, the original case that it is compared to, the specific legal ruling of the original case, and the common cause between the original and the branch. An example of this is the prohibition of leasing during the call to Friday prayer, analogous to the prohibition of selling during the same call due to the shared cause, which is distraction from the Friday prayer. The ruling of the original case and its cause are derived from Allah's saying: {O you who have believed, when the call is announced for the prayer on the day of Friday, hasten to the remembrance of Allah and leave off trade}(37).
Here, the branch is leasing and the original case is selling, and the specific legal ruling of the original case that is applied to the branch is prohibition, meaning the prohibition of selling during the call to Friday prayer, and the cause is distraction from the Friday prayer.
Most discussions on analogy revolve around the cause; if a cause is required for analogy, there is disagreement about whether this cause is the motivator for legislating the specific ruling, or the intent of the legislator behind the ruling, or the wisdom behind legislating the ruling. There is also disagreement about whether it should be derived solely from the text, as indicated by it, or whether reason has a role in deducing or determining it.

Analogy (qiyas) as a principle of legislation is rejected by the Zahiri school and by the Imami Shia. Ibn Hazm vehemently rejected analogy. In the context of the disagreement regarding the cause (illa), al-Amidi(38) says: "They differed on the permissibility of the cause in the original sense being merely an indication; the preferred view is that the cause in the original sense must be understood as a motivator, meaning it must include a valid wisdom that is intended by the Legislator in enacting the ruling."
Some have argued that it is permissible to provide a rationale based on any abstract description, even if the context of the description does not indicate causality. Al-Amidi (may Allah have mercy on him) said(39): "Most scholars have maintained that it is impermissible to justify a ruling based on a wisdom that lacks a defining criterion, while a minority have permitted it. Therefore, Sheikh Muhammad al-Khudari (may Allah have mercy on him) has stated that the rationale is applied in relation to two concepts:
"The first is the wisdom that motivates the legislation of the ruling, which is a benefit sought to be attained or completed, and a harm sought to be averted or minimized"(40). He means here that it is understood from the text.
The second: "What exists is a presumption for the existence of wisdom"(41), and this is when the wisdom is hidden. What is meant here is that there is nothing in the texts that indicates it, neither explicitly nor implicitly.
Others hold that the legal cause (illa) is derived only from the Qur'an, the Sunnah, and the consensus of the Companions (ijma'), and do not see any legal value in the cause unless it is indicated by the texts. "It becomes clear from the examination of the legal texts in the Qur'an and the Sunnah that the legal text indicates the cause either explicitly, implicitly, or through deduction."

Or by analogy, and there is no indication of the legal cause from the Sharia, that is, from the recognized texts as legal texts except for these four conditions and nothing else.
And the correct view – and Allah knows best – is that the cause, which is a pillar in analogy, is that which means the impetus for legislation – it is the address of the legislator either explicitly or implicitly; otherwise, the ruling that is justified by it would not be a legal ruling. It contains the meaning, or it is the meaning for which the original ruling was legislated, and if it is devoid of this meaning, then it cannot be the recognized cause for analogy, even if it is called so; rather, it would then be merely a statement identifying the ruling or indicating it without indicating the reason for which the ruling was legislated.
The cause is the reason for which the ruling was legislated, and it is not merely a wisdom or a mere objective of the ruling. The reason for which the ruling was legislated cannot be known by a person without the legislator informing about it. Therefore, the cause has two conditions:
It must be a reason for legislating the ruling of the original, and not merely a description within it, but a description that is understood to imply causation.
The legislator must specify the reason for legislation or the impetus, and this is derived from the texts and their indications. The statement of anyone saying: "This is the cause" without referring to legal evidence for the cause has no value.
And analogy is a definitive proof, and its evidence is its reality. For although the branch being analogized does not have a text, there is a text in its original, which is the one it is being compared to. If the ruling of the original was legislated for a specific cause, then if the branch contains the same cause, it is subject to the same ruling as the original, because the ruling in the original was legislated for the cause. However, if a text comes that clarifies the ruling of the branch, even if the cause exists, then there is no analogy in that case. It has been "agreed upon that there is no opinion in the presence of a text"(43). That is, there is no analogy.

Objectives of the Sharia

Before discussing the unregulated interests (masalih mursala) in terms of their definition and their status as a principle of the Sharia, it is essential to address some preliminary points that those who advocate for interests as a legal proof rely upon, including the objectives of the Sharia, the pursuit of benefits, and the prevention of harms, as well as the significance of these objectives or interests.

Objectives of the Sharia

Some of the imams of usul al-fiqh (principles of jurisprudence) state that the Sharia has objectives and that it was established to preserve these objectives for creation. Al-Amidi(56) says: "The purpose of establishing a ruling can only be classified as either belonging to the category of necessary objectives or not belonging to the category of necessary objectives. If it belongs to the category of necessary objectives, then it is either a foundational principle or not a foundational principle. If it is a foundational principle, it refers to the five objectives that have been preserved by every community and every law, which are: the preservation of religion, life, intellect, lineage, and wealth. The limitation to these five categories is based on the occurrence and the knowledge of the absence of any necessary objective outside of them in practice... If it is not a foundational principle, it is a complementary aspect of the necessary, such as the emphasis on preserving the intellect by prohibiting the consumption of even a small amount of intoxicants that leads to larger amounts, even if it is not intoxicating. As for if the objective does not belong to the necessary objectives, it is either something that people have a need for or not. If it is something that people have a need for, then it is either a foundational principle or not a foundational principle. If it is a foundational principle, it is the second category related to additional needs, such as granting the guardian authority to marry off a minor... If it is not a foundational principle, it is a supplementary aspect that serves as a completion and addition to the second category, such as considering compatibility and the customary dowry."

The texts indicate that this Sharia has objectives that serve as the purpose of its application to creation. Allah, Exalted is He, said: "And We have not sent you except as a mercy to the worlds"(67). This text negates—by His saying "and We have not"—the idea that the sending of the Messenger has any purpose other than being a mercy to the worlds. The sending of the Messenger (peace and blessings be upon him) was indeed through the message, so the message itself is the mercy, meaning that the existence of this message applied to people is the mercy. If we wish to understand this mercy, we can imagine a society where there is no trace of the application of Islam, and where its systems and laws are of human origin; such laws lead to corruption, injustice, misery, and a life of hardship. Meanwhile, the Sharia of Islam brings people out of corruption into righteousness, from darkness into light, and from misery into prosperity.
And Allah, Exalted is He, said: "And We send down from the Qur'an that which is healing and mercy for the believers"(68). The two verses indicate what the Legislator intends from the message and from the Qur'an, which is healing and mercy, and these are objectives that result from application. They are objectives in the sense of goals, meaning results that may arise from the Sharia or may not arise. The texts do not indicate that the attainment of these results is inevitable, because there are people who reject this message, and there are communities that reject it and do not apply it, but rather fight against it; thus, the goal, or the mercy for which the message was sent, will not be realized among them. The sending of the Messenger as a mercy, and the sending down of the Qur'an as healing, means the intended state that is to exist from the sending of the message and the sending down of the Qur'an, or in other words, that Allah, Exalted is He, has shown mercy to the worlds by sending them the message, or by sending them the medicine or healing remedy they need for their ailments of misery, corruption, and hardship. The Sharia was sent to be a mercy, and there is nothing in the two verses that indicates it was legislated because it is a mercy; rather, the meaning is that its legislation and sending is the mercy.

The Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) said: "Whoever comes to you while your command is united upon one man and seeks to split your ranks or divide your community, kill him"(82). He also said: "If allegiance is given to two caliphs, kill the latter of the two"(83).

These objectives are known by the texts of the legislator and are not known by reason. They are ends. Our definition of them as ends is as understood directly from the text. The end is the end of legislation. The objectives of the Sharia are the ends of its legislation. The objectives of rulings are the ends of the legislation of rulings. The end of legislating a specific ruling is determined by the one who legislated the ruling, which is Allah, Exalted is He. Reason cannot understand them except through Allah informing about them. As for what some claim about understanding the objectives of legislation through induction, this is not correct for several reasons:
The rulings for which the legislator has specified objectives differ in their objectives, so each ruling has an objective different from the other. If there is a ruling for which the legislator has not specified its objective, we cannot determine the objective of the legislator from it based on the objectives of other rulings, as they are different.
The legislator is Allah, and He cannot be compared to a human. If the legislator were a human, we could, to some extent, grasp the reality of his essence, motivations, and mentality as a human, and thus we could speculate about his objective or objectives. However, since the legislator is Allah, Exalted is He, we cannot grasp anything of this, and thus we cannot know His objective unless He informs us about it.
The scholars have agreed that there are rulings for which reason cannot discern their objectives without Allah informing about them, and these are the rulings of acts of worship. Every ruling for which the legislator has not informed about its objective is like them. It should not be said that this is specific to acts of worship, because acts of worship are part of the Sharia and are commands and prohibitions like other rulings. The classification of the rulings of the Sharia into acts of worship, transactions, and others does not give acts of worship a specificity that is not shared by the rulings of transactions or ethics or others, because this classification is from humans and not based on evidence from the Sharia itself. This classification is correct.


[Page 39]

In terms of the reality of these rulings. The acts of worship are the set of rulings that regulate the relationship between a person and their Creator, while the rulings of transactions are the set of rulings that regulate the relationship between a person and other human beings. Ethics are the rulings that regulate a person's relationship with themselves, so to speak, or rather: they regulate a person's private conduct, such as their eating, clothing, and the refinement of their inclinations, nature, and temperament. Similarly, there is a classification that categorizes certain things: food, drinks, clothing, or: measured items, counted items, and weighted items; this is a classification of things based on their reality, and no category has any advantage or specificity over another except by evidence from the Sharia. This classification is akin to codifying the rulings of the Sharia and making them systems, such as an economic system, a social system, a governance system, a penal system, and a system or rulings of clear evidence; these are different systems in terms of their reality or in terms of the nature of what they address. However, they are also one system, which is the system of Islam, and therefore, the rulings of the economic system, for example, cannot be given a specific description that is not applicable to other systems unless the texts indicate so. The same applies to the rulings of acts of worship. The lack of seeking objectives for them should apply to all rulings. This is one aspect, and on the other hand, all rulings of the Sharia are acts of worship, and a person must worship Allah in all their actions, as He, Exalted is He, said: “And I did not create the jinn and mankind except to worship Me”(84). Just as prayer is an act of worship, truthfulness is an act of worship, obedience to the ruler is an act of worship, jihad is an act of worship, avoiding alcohol is an act of worship, and refraining from usury is an act of worship.
The intellect may sometimes grasp an objective or objectives in the sense of a wisdom or wisdoms behind a particular ruling, but it cannot determine that this is the wisdom behind legislating that ruling; what it has grasped in terms of wisdoms—despite its importance—may be secondary compared to the wisdom of others that it has not comprehended due to the limitations of its knowledge or its lack of insight into the unseen. Therefore, the intellect cannot determine the wisdom, even though it can.

Bringing Benefits and Preventing Harms

Some scholars of usul (principles of jurisprudence) and writers in the field have said that the objectives of the Sharia (maqasid al-sharia) and the objectives of rulings are to bring benefits to people and to prevent harms from them. Al-Amidi, for example, says(85): "... we know from the state of the Legislator that He does not issue a ruling devoid of wisdom, as rulings are established for the benefits of the servants, and this is not by necessity, but rather by considering the customary practice of legislating rulings." His statement that it is not by necessity is contrary to the view of the Mu'tazila, and his statement regarding considering the customary practice means by extrapolating the rulings.

He also says(86): "The purpose of legislating a ruling is either to bring a benefit or to avert a harm, or a combination of both concerning the servant, as the Exalted Lord is above harm and benefit, and perhaps this is intended for the servant because it is suitable for him and aligns with his nature." The suitable here means appropriate, and as Abu Zayd(87) said, as quoted by Al-Amidi(88): "The appropriate is that which, if presented to minds, would be accepted." However, Al-Amidi believes that it is more accurate to say(89): "The appropriate is a clear, defined description that necessitates that arranging the ruling accordingly results in what is suitable to be intended by legislating that ruling." Al-Amidi stipulates that there must be a text that attests to the consideration of the benefit or the intended purpose or the appropriate, otherwise the consideration is not valid. He states(90): "The appropriate that does not have a foundational text from the principles of the Sharia attesting to its consideration... nor has its abrogation appeared."

The appropriateness

Al-Shawkani(102) said: "The appropriateness is the essence of the book of analogy (qiyas) and the place of its ambiguity and clarity, and extracting it is called the extraction of the legal basis (takhrij al-manaat)." Al-Shawkani attributed to al-Mahsool: "People have mentioned two things in defining appropriateness: the first is that it leads to what is beneficial for a person in terms of acquisition and preservation, and it may be expressed in terms of acquisition as bringing benefit and in terms of preservation as repelling harm. The second is that it is suitable for the actions of rational beings in customs, as it is said that this pearl is appropriate for this pearl in bringing them together in a compatible string."(103)

Some have defined appropriateness as that which, if presented to minds, would be accepted. Abu Zayd al-Dabbusi said this, as did al-Shatibi. Ibn Qudamah al-Maqdisi mentioned that appropriateness is the attribute that, when establishing a ruling, leads to a benefit(104). Ali al-Shashi gave an example and said(105): "If we see a person giving a poor man a dirham, it is likely that the giving is to meet the need of the poor and to acquire the reward." From this, it can be inferred that meeting the need and acquiring the reward is an appropriate description and thus serves as the reason for the giving.

Al-Shawkani quotes al-Razi saying(106): "Appropriateness is what entails acquiring a benefit or repelling a harm; the former is like wealth being the reason for the obligation of zakat, and the latter is like intoxication being the reason for the prohibition of wine." He also quotes Ibn al-Hajib's definition of appropriateness(107): "Appropriateness is an apparent, well-defined attribute that is rationally derived from the arrangement of the ruling upon it, which is suitable to be intended for acquiring a benefit or repelling a harm." All these definitions convey a similar meaning, approximately.

The Considered Interests

We have previously encountered al-Amidi's expression for it as the "considered interest," and the two common expressions are: considered interests (masalih mursala) and istislah. We have also noted that considered interests are those interests that the Sharia has neither acknowledged nor rejected. They are actions intended to bring about a benefit or avert a harm, and they have no evidence from the Sharia. Al-Shawkani (may Allah have mercy on him) said(117): "Ibn Burhan stated that they are based on a general principle and not a specific one." Istislah is said to be the pursuit of reform, and it is also said to be the pursuit of goodness. In legal terminology, it refers to deriving a legal ruling based on an interest, with the addition that the interest must fall within the objectives of the Lawgiver (Shari). Al-Shawkani (may Allah have mercy on him) said(118): "Some have called it 'the inference from the considered interest,' and Imam al-Haramayn and Ibn al-Sam'ani referred to it as inference."

In the preceding discussion of this research, there is enough to clarify how to infer the position on considered interests, and the following is a summary of the argument, which serves as evidence for the validity of considered interests.

The Sharia has objectives, and it has been established through induction that these objectives are to bring about benefits and avert harms. If this is established through the examination of the texts of the Sharia, it becomes a general principle, and it is applied to specific cases that do not have a text or specific evidence, that is, to what has not been included in the examination. The issues that lack a text or consensus are considered based on the benefits or harms they produce, and a legal ruling is given based on this consideration. The absence of a detailed evidence indicating the ruling does not mean that the ruling based on bringing about a benefit or averting a harm is not Sharia-compliant, as it has evidence that may be stronger than the detailed evidence, which is the general evidence derived through induction. This understanding is the cornerstone of the position of all those who advocate for it.

The Position of Scholars on Public Interest (Maslaha)

I almost titled this section "The Scholars' Rejection of Public Interest" instead. For no one can be said to have accepted it without reservation. However, it is often attributed to Malik and the Malikis, and many contemporary scholars advocate for it. Dr. al-Bagha(137) states: "The words of the usuliyyin (jurisprudents) almost converge on the fact that the opinion of public interest is a matter of disagreement, and the prevailing view is that it is not valid for inference, as there is no evidence to support its consideration, and only Imam Malik (may Allah have mercy on him) has adopted this view."

Some contemporary proponents of public interest argue that the four imams have indeed accepted it, despite reports of their rejection of public interest—except for Malik. They also claim that the Companions (may Allah be pleased with them) accepted it. Al-Bouti(138) states: "Public interests are accepted by consensus among the Companions, the Tabi'in (Successors), and the four imams." These conclusions and opinions contradict the transmitted texts; so where is the truth? Furthermore, the conclusions of contemporary scholars contradict the clear and explicit reports from the imams, as reported from Imam Ahmad ibn Hanbal, and even what they themselves stated, such as al-Ghazali in al-Mustasfa. What, then, is the truth?

The truth is that if a correct narration is reported from any of them stating that they do not accept public interest, we must affirm what they said, unless it becomes clear that they have abandoned that statement. However, to delve into their jurisprudential branches and search for some rulings, and if we find some of them for which we do not know the evidence, to hastily claim: "Its evidence was public interest," is not correct at all, nor does it hold any validity in reasoning. The statement of those who say, "Public interests are accepted by consensus among the Companions, the Tabi'in, and the four imams," is a bold claim that has no justification other than a disregard for knowledge and a reckless approach to expressing opinions.

And moving away from ambiguity, I will present below some of the opinions of the scholars, and I will present the positions that it is claimed the Companions (may Allah be pleased with them) took regarding public interest (maslaha) in its unregulated form.
As for the positions of the imams and scholars, the majority hold that public interest (maslaha) in its unregulated form is not a legal proof. Imam al-Shafi'i (may Allah be pleased with him) rejected this view. Al-Amidi reported this from him. Many scholars of the Shafi'i school, such as al-Amidi, al-Ghazali, and Izz al-Din ibn Abd al-Salam, also reported his rejection. It is well-known that al-Shafi'i said: "Whoever deems something good has legislated." This condemned deeming good, according to al-Shafi'i, includes the public interest (maslaha) intended by the Malikis.
Imam al-Shafi'i says in his Risala: "Indeed, deeming good is a form of pleasure," and then he states: "The scholar should not speak except from the perspective of knowledge, and the perspective of knowledge is the necessary report by analogy with the evidence of what is correct, so that the scholar is always following a report, and the seeker of knowledge is by analogy," and he said: "If he speaks without a necessary report or analogy, he is closer to sin than the one who speaks while being ignorant..." He also said: "And Allah has not allowed anyone after the Messenger of Allah to speak except from the perspective of knowledge that has preceded him, and the perspective of knowledge thereafter is the Book, the Sunnah, consensus, and the reports, and what I have described of analogy upon them"(139).
From these statements of Imam al-Shafi'i (may Allah be pleased with him), we understand that he strongly criticizes public interest (maslaha) in its unregulated form just as he criticizes deeming good, and indeed the former is included in the latter for him, as it is neither a necessary report nor an analogy since it is unregulated. Therefore, al-Ghazali said the same as al-Shafi'i. He said: "Indeed, whoever deems something good has legislated just as whoever deems something good has legislated"(140).
Public interest (maslaha) in its unregulated form is not considered by Imam Ahmad ibn Hanbal (may Allah be pleased with him), and it is not mentioned among the principles he relied upon. Scholars of his school, such as Ibn Taymiyyah and Ibn Qudamah al-Maqdisi, have rejected it. As for what some contemporary scholars claim that he follows Imam Malik in accepting it(141), this is a statement for which there is no evidence.

The first evidence: The legislator has established the genus of interests within the genus of rulings

This is their first evidence and it is the cornerstone of their arguments. It is based on the premise that the legislator has objectives that are the interests of creation, and that the original principle in the Sharia is to consider these interests(179).
Indeed, the Sharia has objectives, as indicated by the Sharia itself, and these objectives pertain to the Sharia as a whole and not to each individual ruling. This is evidenced by the texts that imply that the Sharia has objectives, such as His saying: “And We have not sent you, [O Muhammad], except as a mercy to the worlds” {وما أرسلناك إلا رحمة للعالمين}(180), and His saying: “And We send down in the Qur'an that which is healing and mercy for the believers” {وننزل من القرآن ما هو شفاء ورحمة للمؤمنين}(181), and His saying: “This is a guidance for the people and a mercy for those who are certain” {هذا بصائر للناس وهدى ورحمة لقوم يوقنون}(182). D- The establishment of the diwan (divisions) by Umar and their organization and arrangement so that they encompass all the interests of the state, and his taking of the prison and its dating by the Hijra(214).
These actions were not guided by public interest (maslaha), but rather aimed at achieving a result that can be obtained, which is the public interest. As for the evidence, it is the Sharia, and these actions are viewed as follows:
When the Islamic state expanded and the number of Muslims increased during the time of Umar—who is the guardian of the Muslims and responsible for their care—their conditions and affairs became in need of regulation, preservation, and documentation, in order to manage their affairs according to the rulings of the Sharia. Therefore, Umar introduced these means and methods to fulfill the duty of implementing Islam. If the implementation of Islam and the care of the Muslims' affairs can only be achieved through these methods and means or similar ones, then adopting them or adopting similar ones becomes obligatory according to the legal principle: "What is necessary to complete an obligation is itself an obligation." Thus, the evidence for the action is the Sharia, while the benefit is the desired outcome. On one hand, the Sharia has indicated the permissibility of these things that Umar introduced, as the default ruling for things is permissibility unless there is evidence for prohibition. For example, the Sharia has commanded the collection of funds from zakat, kharaj, jizyah, and others, and has mandated their distribution in specific ways. If the funds become abundant and the sources and expenditures multiply, and there are many who are obligated to pay zakat, kharaj, or a tithe, then organization becomes obligatory for the complete implementation of Islam, not merely for the sake of benefit. As for the use of specific things or certain means and methods over others for this organization and care, the choice falls on what best achieves the desired outcome as seen by the one in charge of care, which is the caliph or imam. However, he is obliged to choose from what Allah has permitted and to avoid what He has prohibited, even if it is found that the latter is more effective in achieving the desired outcome. These things that Umar introduced are permissible based on the principle of the permissibility of things, as there is no evidence for their prohibition. The actions that were carried out regarding these things are the implementation of Islam itself.

H - The Inclusion of Craftsmen(216)

Al-Shatibi stated this, and among the modern scholars, al-Bouti mentioned it and attributed the practice to the Companions and the Followers based on public interest (maslaha). Some writers in the field of usul (principles of jurisprudence) have counted the inclusion of craftsmen as an example of istihsan (juridical preference). Al-Bouti mentioned that Abu Hanifa (may Allah be pleased with him) permits istisna (manufacturing) based on public interest, which is incorrect and indeed dismissive. For Abu Hanifa advocates istihsan and rejects public interest in the view of those who established it on its branches. Regardless, istisna is permissible and established by the Sunnah, not by public interest or istihsan. The Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) had a ring made(217) and had a pulpit made. People used to have things made during the time of the Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him), and he remained silent about them, so his silence is an endorsement of istisna(218). As for the inclusion of craftsmen, the subject of the contract in the istisna process is what is made, such as a ring, a pulpit, a cabinet, a car, and so on. In this regard, it is akin to a sale. However, if a person brings raw materials to the craftsman and asks him to make something, then it becomes a matter of rental (ijara). Therefore, to determine the legal ruling regarding inclusion, one must consider the basis for its realization, whether it is akin to a sale or a rental, and then apply the relevant legal rulings. If it is a sale, the craftsman is liable. If it is a rental, there is no liability on the craftsman, as the Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) said: "Whoever entrusts a trust, there is no liability upon him"(219). It was also reported from Ali (may Allah be pleased with him) that he said: "There is no liability on a shepherd or on a trustee"(220). If the craftsman is given the material to make something from it or with it, he is a trustee or an honest keeper, and the material with him is a trust whether it is fabric, wood, iron, or otherwise.

What did the scholars mean by "public interest" (maslaha)?

We have discussed public interest (maslaha) as a source of legislation and demonstrated with evidence that it does not stand on its own as proof. We have refuted with evidence and argument all that those who advocate for public interest have imagined to be proof of its validity. The impetus for this discussion was the widespread notion among some of the later scholars of usul (principles of jurisprudence) that public interest is a legislative source, and that public interest serves as evidence for legal rulings. Wherever there is public interest, there is the law of Allah, and the principles derived from this have been established as legal principles, whereas they are nothing but the establishment of public interest as a religion. In fact, some of the later scholars resort to the writings of the imams and the predecessors, extracting texts or fatwas and presenting them to us in meanings other than those intended by the imams. Therefore, it is necessary to warn against the ideas of these individuals, who confuse Muslims regarding the rulings of their religion, and make public interest a grand title through which they seek to free themselves from the constraints and rulings of religion. Thus, it was essential to refute their arguments and claims, and clarify the truth in this matter. Consequently, we have clarified the position of the scholars regarding public interest, and since al-Shatibi and al-Qarafi have endorsed it, it is necessary to explain the true meaning of their endorsement of public interest.

-Upon closer examination, it becomes clear that no one has claimed that public interest serves as evidence for action—anyone who attributes such a statement to someone is required to provide evidence without distortion or misrepresentation. Those who have spoken in favor of public interest merely intended that a person may choose within the realm of permissible actions according to their own interest or benefit or what is most suitable for them, and there is no harm in this. They have discussed the means and methods that were present during the time of the Messenger (peace and blessings be upon him). The means are subject to the principle of original permissibility, which is: the default ruling of things is permissibility unless there is evidence of prohibition. The methods depend on the action intended to be performed and the available or possible means for it. Thus, the methods can vary, change, and develop according to the variety, change, and development of the possible means. If the means are permissible, and the action intended to be performed is permissible, then the methods will take on the ruling of permissibility or the ruling of


[Page 102]

The action in detail is not what we are addressing here. As an example for clarification: we take jihad as an example; it is an obligation, and it has means, including the sword, spear, shield, and horse, as well as the tank, airplane, cannon, and others. All of these are permissible things in combat. The use of the sword and spear and similar weapons necessitates combat methods that differ from those required by modern weapons. Likewise, the transmission of news, which used to rely on humans and horses or on pigeons, has changed; the means have transformed and the methods have evolved, so that news is now transmitted via telephone, fax, or others. These methods are governed by the same ruling as the means or the ruling of the commanded action, provided there is no other method. Another example is the election of the caliph, which may occur through consulting the people of authority and resolution (ahl al-hall wa al-aqd), or through consulting the entire community; whichever method is used, it is permissible as long as it expresses the consent and choice of the community. The opinion of the people may be gathered through someone who collects their views, or by raising hands to determine the majority, or through secret ballot boxes. All of these are methods, and they are permissible if the action conducted through them is permissible, and the means used are not prohibited.
However, if the term "public interest" (maslaha) refers to the existence of a legislative source such as the Qur'an, the Sunnah, the consensus of the Companions, and analogy (qiyas), then this cannot be established in any case. Rather, it is acceptable if it refers to new means and innovative methods, for which there is evidence in the sources of legislation indicating them or their origin, that is, what they are considered a branch of.

The opinion of Imam al-Shatibi

He, may Allah have mercy on him, said in al-I'tisam(272): "The essence of the unregulated interests (masalih mursala) returns to preserving a necessary matter and alleviating a necessary hardship in religion. Also, its reference is to preserving the necessary from the perspective of 'what is not completed without it...' Thus, it is a means and not an objective, and its return to alleviating hardship pertains to easing, not tightening." So, the statement of Imam al-Shatibi: "a means" clarifies what we have stated, and his statement: "not an objective" is a response to anyone who claims that the existence of something as an interest is evidence of its permissibility. For the objectives are the results of actions, and the ruling of actions is from the Sharia, but the means to the action is what is taken based on the unregulated interest.

Al-Shatibi believes that the inference from interest regarding a means should be in the case of the absence of a text on that means, and the absence of analogy (qiyas) as well. He said(273): "The unregulated interests refer to considering the appropriate that does not have a specific origin supporting it, so it does not have a specific legal witness, nor is it an analogy such that if presented to minds, it would be accepted." Here, the importance of his statement "specific legal witness" appears, meaning that this means or method does not have a specific text, otherwise it would not fall under the unregulated interests, and also that there is no analogy indicating that means or method. He also said(274): "As for it being in the necessary matters as a means and 'what is not completed without it,' if it is stipulated, then it is a legal condition, and thus it has no place in this regard, because the text of the legislator has relieved us from considering it. If it is not stipulated, then it is either rational or customary, and it is not necessary for it to be legal. Likewise, it is not necessary for it to be in a known manner." Thus, it becomes clear from what he said that the unregulated interest is a means or method.

There is no specific or particular text that indicates this, but there is a general principle or comprehensive evidence under which the means or methods fall. It divides them into two categories: entities and actions. The entities are means to which the ruling of the original principle in things applies. The actions are methods that are subject to the rule that what is necessary for the completion of an obligation is itself obligatory.
And his statement: "And if it is not stipulated, then it is either rational or customary, so it does not have to be legal" refers to the fact that the ruling based on public interest (maslaha) may have its evidence in "what is necessary for the completion of an obligation is itself obligatory," and this is a necessary principle in both legal and non-legal matters. The public interest (maslaha mursala) according to al-Shatibi does not mean that it is unrestrictedly sent forth, but rather that it refers to means or methods that are derived from a specific evidence or particular proof that directly indicates this detail, but they are not derived from a general evidence or comprehensive proof under which they fall. He stated in al-Muwafaqat(275): "Every legal principle that does not have a specific text supporting it, and is compatible with the actions of the Sharia and whose meaning is taken from its evidences, is valid and can be relied upon if that principle has become certain through its collective evidences. Because the evidences do not have to indicate certainty in the ruling by themselves without the addition of others to them, as previously mentioned, because that is nearly impossible, and this includes a type of unrestricted reasoning..."
What reinforces this meaning of public interests (masalih mursala) according to al-Shatibi is his statement(276): "If we were to assume the preservation of the Qur'an and knowledge without books, that would be valid, and likewise, all other necessary interests can be preserved." For the writing of the mushaf and the writing of knowledge, such as hadith and jurisprudence, are among the necessary methods for preserving the Qur'an and preserving knowledge. If we were to assume that the preservation of the Qur'an and knowledge is possible through a method other than writing, that would be acceptable legally. It is as if al-Shatibi is saying: the preservation of the Qur'an and the preservation of knowledge is an obligation, and it is necessary to adopt the required method for that, even if there is no specific evidence stipulated for it. The absence of a specific text makes that method a public interest (maslaha mursala), and if there is only one method, then that method is obligatory based on "what is necessary for the completion of an obligation is itself obligatory," and if there are multiple methods, then any one of them that is adopted is valid.

He believes that the premise of the existence of a caliph is indicated by the text, and it is supported by the principle that what is necessary for the fulfillment of an obligation is itself an obligation. As for the principle, it is because Islam cannot be implemented without a caliph, and since implementing Islam is an obligation, the existence of a caliph is also an obligation. This is in the case where there is no text on the imamate.
Al-Shatibi does not see that the public interest is what is determined by reason; rather, the public interest is the action commanded by the legislator. If we know that an action is commanded by the legislator, we then know that it is a public interest, and likewise, we know that what leads to it is also a public interest. The same applies to corruption (mafsada); the public interest is what the Sharia has deemed to be a public interest, not what the intellect, desires, or self inclines towards. He says: "The public interests established by Sharia and the evils repelled are only considered in terms of how this life supports the afterlife, not in terms of the whims of souls in seeking their ordinary interests or repelling their ordinary evils." The opinion of Imam al-Qarafi

As for what al-Qarafi has stated, it indicates that he did not intend by the public interest (maslaha) anything far removed from what al-Shatibi intended, and that he did not aim to make public interest a source of legislation. Those who rejected public interest did so as a principle among the foundational principles or as a legal ruling that has no evidence whatsoever, neither partial nor general, as is understood from the term "public". Due to the difference in the meaning of public interest between its rejecters and its proponents, it is necessary to investigate this matter. Al-Qarafi finds it strange that the view of the public interest is attributed to Malik and the Malikis, while it is rejected by others, whereas, in reality, everyone agrees with it—according to his opinion. He says(281): "What is unknown is the public interest, which we affirm, and upon investigation, it is general among the schools of thought." He said(282): "And it is, according to Malik, a proof." He said(283): "As for public interest, it is reported that it is specific to us, and if the schools of thought are lacking, they find that when they make analogies and distinguish between the two issues, they do not seek a witness by way of consideration for that meaning by which they gathered and distinguished, but rather they suffice with the mere analogy, and this is public interest, so it is then present in all schools of thought." He provided examples of public interest from the actions of the Companions (may Allah be pleased with them), expressing his astonishment that the Malikis are denied the right to speak of it, rather he said that those who denied it took it into account. This indicates the difference in the meaning he intends from the meaning that they denied. He said(284): "It has been previously established that public interest is present in all schools of thought upon investigation because they make analogies and distinguish by means of analogies and do not seek a witness by way of consideration, and we do not mean by public interest anything other than that. What further confirms the practice of public interest is that the Companions (may Allah be pleased with them) undertook matters for the sake of pure public interest without a prior witness by way of consideration, such as the compilation of the Qur'an, for which there was no prior command or analogy, and the appointment of the caliphate.

From Abu Bakr to Umar (may Allah be pleased with them), there was no prior command or precedent in this matter, and likewise, the caliphate was left to consultation (shura), the recording of accounts (dawawin), the minting of currency for the Muslims, and the establishment of prisons were actions taken by Umar (may Allah be pleased with him). The demolition of the endowments (awqaf) adjacent to the Mosque of the Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) and the expansion of the mosque when it became cramped was done by Uthman (may Allah be pleased with him). The renewal of the call to prayer (adhan) on Friday in the marketplace, which is the first adhan, was also done by Uthman (may Allah be pleased with him), and this is very much in line with absolute public interest (maslaha). The Imam of the Two Holy Sanctuaries (Imam al-Haramayn) has addressed matters in his book titled al-Ghayathi, permitting them and issuing fatwas on them, while the Malikis have distanced themselves from them and have bridged over them, stating them for absolute public interest. Likewise, al-Ghazali in al-Shifa al-Ghalil, although both of them strongly denied our stance on public interest (maslaha mursala).
We know that those who rejected public interests (masalih mursala) did so on the grounds that they lack specific or general evidence, whether partial or total. Al-Qarafi's statement that they acted upon them, along with the examples he provided, indicates that he means by "mursala" that it is unbound from specific evidence for that very action and not from general or total evidence. Precisely, it is for him a matter that the intellect understands to be subject to general or total evidence, thus falling under that evidence without there being specific evidence for it. The evidence by consideration that al-Qarafi speaks of is what attests to the intellect's correctness in understanding or judging that matter to fall under that evidence. In this sense, his statement about its existence in all schools of thought is valid; in fact, this is inevitable. If every ruling required the intellect to have evidence, no ruling would be established in this world, and certainty in the foundation of religion would not exist.
Thus, there is no basis for anyone to attribute to these two imams their stance on public interest as a source of legislation, or that the presence of public interest in an action indicates its permissibility. A further examination of al-Qarafi's statements or their statements confirms this meaning, so there is no need for lengthy elaboration.

The opinion of a group of contemporary scholars

A large number of our contemporary scholars have gone to the view that the term "public interest (maslaha) that is not explicitly mentioned" is a name that some scholars of the principles of Sharia have sometimes used to refer to the means and methods by which legislative rulings are implemented, and this reinforces the understanding we have presented regarding the imams' views on public interest that is not explicitly mentioned.
This opinion has become clear in the memorandum submitted by a crowd of scholars from the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia to King Fahd under the name "Memorandum of Advice," which was published in 1993. I will mention here some excerpts from what the magazine reported and what was included in the memorandum to clarify the scholars' opinions and the content of the opinion.
The magazine stated: "... the materials or procedural rulings, and these are the materials that stipulate rulings related to material means and methods by which legislative rulings are implemented, which is what scholars of the principles of Sharia have sometimes referred to as public interest that is not explicitly mentioned." The scholars then provided examples of public interest that is not explicitly mentioned. The opinion of Imam Izz al-Din ibn Abd al-Salam

Imam Izz al-Din ibn Abd al-Salam rejects legislation based on public interest (maslaha) that is not explicitly established. For him, public interest is defined by legal rulings. He states(288): "Most of the interests and harms of this world are known by reason," then he says(289): "There is no comparison between the interests and harms of this world and those of the Hereafter," and then he says(290): "As for the interests and causes of both worlds and their harms, they can only be known through the Sharia." Therefore, his conclusion is that interests and harms can only be known through the Sharia, because the interests and harms of this world, that is, interests based on personal desire or rational determination, are insignificant compared to the interests and harms of the Hereafter. The interests and harms of the Hereafter can only be known through transmission. Thus, the interest is what the Sharia commands, and the harm is what the Sharia prohibits. This is the ultimate statement regarding the non-recognition of public interest as a source or foundation for legislation. This clarifies that his use of the term public interest does not indicate interests based on opinion, desire, or reason; rather, for him, public interest is the legal ruling and what the legal ruling necessitates. In fact, if matters are left to reason, it may determine interests or harms that are contrary to what is established in the Sharia, and consideration is only for the Sharia. Therefore, you will not find any importance in his view in labeling something as harm or interest; rather, some things that the Sharia commands he may label as harm, and some things that the Sharia prohibits he may label as interest. For example, he states(291): "Sometimes the causes of interests are harms, and they may be commanded or permitted not because they are harms but because they lead to interests, such as cutting off the hands of those who steal to preserve lives, and risking lives in jihad. Likewise, all legal punishments are not sought because they are harms but because they are intended by their legislation, such as cutting off the hand of the thief, executing the highway robber, killing the criminals, stoning the adulterers, and exiling them, as well as the discretionary punishments. The Opinion of Imam al-Ghazali

Imam al-Ghazali's understanding of public interest (maslaha) aligns with that of Imam Izz al-Din ibn Abd al-Salam. He does not see public interest as what is deemed beneficial by reason or humanity; he states: "As for public interest, it is originally about bringing benefit or averting harm, and we do not mean that. For bringing benefit and averting harm are the objectives of creation and the welfare of creation in achieving their objectives, but we mean by public interest the preservation of the objectives of the Sharia, and the objectives of the Sharia concerning creation are five: to preserve their religion, their lives, their intellect, their lineage, and their wealth"(295).

He rejects istislah (the practice of deriving legal rulings based on public interest) as a source, categorizing it under the title: "Clarification that there are things thought to be among the sources of evidence, but are not, and they are also four: the law of those before us, the sayings of the companions, istihsan (juridical preference), and istislah"(296).

He concludes his remarks on istislah(297): "Istislah is not a fifth source in itself, but whoever uses istislah has legislated just as whoever uses istihsan has legislated." These statements from Imam al-Ghazali are clear and explicit in not considering istislah a source of legislation.

However, some, as previously mentioned in our research, attempt to argue that public interest is a recognized source among the imams, scholars, and jurists, citing their statements in ways that do not clarify the reality of the matter, leading the reader and those interested in this issue into confusion due to the conflicting statements.

What is clear from al-Ghazali's statements as found in the Mustasfa is that the meaning of public interest (maslaha mursala) for him differs from the meaning of istislah. Istislah is legislation based on the public interest as perceived or determined by reason, and this

Refuting the Two Objections

The first objection: that there are recognized interests, nullified interests, and interests for which the Sharia has given neither recognition nor nullification.

Let us assume here that the prevention of harm (mafsada) falls within the meaning of interest (maslaha). The gist of the statement above is that the Sharia was laid down only to secure interests, or that interests are the motives for legislation and the causes (ʿilal) of rulings. But since interests are something that the intellect estimates, that is, measures by human standards, which are subject to error and correctness and to everything that Allah has created in human nature, it was found, when the rulings were examined, that they did not all come in accordance with the interests as estimated. This alone was enough to demolish this claim at its foundation for those who held that interests are the causes of the Sharia. Instead, however, they said of the rational interests they found contradicting a text of the Sharia that they are nullified interests, and of what they found by their intellects to agree with the texts of the Sharia that it is a recognized interest.
It is in the nature of this approach, that is, holding interests to be causes and dividing them into recognized and nullified interests, that legislative inquiry ends up determining the interest from the action first, and then searching for evidence to serve it. If some meaning or suitable attribute is found in a text that fits the determined interest, the action is judged permissible and the interest recognized. If a text is found that prohibits the action leading to that interest, the action is judged prohibited, or the interest is judged not recognized. But since the rulings of the Sharia are attached to the actions of people and not to what they call an interest, they did not find, for many of the interests they determined, a text that indicates them or aims at them. Nor did they find a text that forbids them. So they said that the Sharia has given them neither recognition nor nullification, and some said that they have no ruling in the Sharia. An example is that they found no text indicating the interest resulting from the compilation of the Qur'an, no text indicating the interest intended in establishing the registers (diwans), and no text that nullifies any


[Page 120]

Either way, they believed that there is no evidence in the Sharia for the two actions. And since there must be evidence for an action to be considered Sharia-compliant, they established a proof they called: the interest (maslaha) that has no evidence, which is the unregulated interest (maslaha mursala).
Indeed, it is possible that there is no evidence indicating the interest in an action or in many actions, because the evidences are not directed towards interests. However, there are evidences that clarify the rulings of these actions. Therefore, the error lies in the foundation. This error is making interests the motives for rulings and their causes. This claim is refuted by the notion of nullified interests, and subsequently, it loses its meaning by the notion of interests that are neither recognized nor nullified. Its implication is that the Sharia is incomplete, which contradicts His saying, Exalted is He: “Today I have completed for you your religion.”
Then, if the rulings indicate interests, what can be said about the nullified interests? Is it not correct to say that they are evils (mafsada), based on the principle that the origin of legislation is the interests of the servants? Therefore, if the Sharia nullifies them, they must be evils. The result is that these interests are evils, meaning that these interests, according to rational estimation, are evils by the ruling of the Sharia. If this is the case, there is nothing preventing us from saying about what they referred to as interests that have no evidence for recognition or nullification that they are evils that have no evidence for recognition or nullification.
Thus, this claim of recognized interests, nullified interests, and interests that are neither recognized nor nullified is of no benefit and cannot serve as a basis for any meaning as long as the Sharia has not indicated interests, and as long as these interests may be evils.

The second objection: Restricting the interest so that it does not contradict a text, consensus, or analogy
As a result of rejecting unregulated interests and not considering them among most of the scholars and jurists, those who advocate for them tend to impose restrictions or criteria on them, including that they must be in accordance with the objectives of the legislator, and they must not contradict the Qur'an, nor the Sunnah, nor consensus, nor analogy, so that they can claim that they are not absolute interests, mistakenly believing that this way they become legitimate.


[Page 121]

As for the statement that it must be in accordance with the objectives of the Lawgiver (maqasid al-sharia), we have previously clarified that the objective of the Lawgiver, which is the public interest (maslaha) according to them, is not the same as the ruling. Rather, it is a result of the application of the ruling, which may occur or may not occur.
However, if what is intended is that which is indicated by the Sharia (Islamic law) and not by reason, then there is no harm in calling it a public interest (maslaha), benefit, good deed, or something else. This is not an unqualified public interest (maslaha mursala), but rather it is an objective of the ruling indicated by the Sharia. It may be considered a cause (illah) depending on the differing opinions of the imams and usuliyyin (jurisprudents) regarding the paths of causation.
And their saying that it - the public interest (maslaha) - must not contradict a text or consensus, nor contradict the Qur'an, nor the Sunnah, nor consensus, nor analogy, as al-Bouti elaborated in his book(306), does not make it legitimate. Because the action that Islam approves is not merely one that does not contradict any of these four sources, but rather one that emerges from them or from some of them. The ruling is the address of the legislator, so it must come in an address to be considered legitimate, and it is not merely that which the legislator has not prohibited or condemned. Then, the legal rulings are five: the obligatory (fard), which is what the legislator has commanded to be done with a decisive demand; the recommended (mandub), which is what the legislator has commanded to be done with a non-decisive demand; the permissible (mubah), which is what the legislator has left to choice between action and inaction; the disliked (makruh), which is what the legislator has commanded to be abandoned with a non-decisive demand; and the forbidden (haram), which is what the legislator has commanded to be abandoned with a decisive demand. So where does the action that does not contradict the Qur'an, nor the Sunnah, nor consensus, nor analogy fall?
There is a speaker who says: If we know that it is not contrary, we know that it is permissible; it may be obligatory, recommended, permissible, or disliked. It is neither forbidden nor something that the legislator has commanded to be abandoned.
I say: The Sharia encompasses all occurrences of life in every time and place, and it provides a ruling for every action of the servants. No human being can encompass all that the Sharia indicates; that is, no person—no matter how knowledgeable—can know everything that is permitted or everything that is prohibited among actions. Therefore, if someone says about a certain action: "I do not find a text indicating its prohibition," this is not knowledge of permissibility, but rather ignorance of the ruling, for it may be that

Therefore, the Sharia encompasses rulings for all occurrences, and silence does not mean the absence of a ruling or the absence of clarification. Regarding the silence on prohibition specifically concerning the general evidence of permissibility, the texts indicate that the default ruling for things is permissibility unless there is evidence of prohibition. This is not silence about clarifying the ruling; rather, it is silence about restricting the ruling, that is, silence about providing additional clarification. If the legislator does not specify the type of neck that must be freed, then any neck would suffice. The absence of specification here is considered a concession.
An example of this is also found in the Noble Qur'an in His saying, Exalted is He: {Indeed, Allah commands you to slaughter a cow}(312). If they slaughtered it without any question, their slaughtering of any cow would suffice. If they slaughtered a cow that was a mature one, or a young one, or not yellow, or had spots, meaning it did not conform to the descriptions that were later requested of them, their action would still be sufficient, and their act would be excused. Thus, the lack of specification here does not pertain to the existence of a case with no ruling; rather, it pertains to the lack of additional detail and clarification of what has already been clarified.

On this basis, the position of the Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) is understood when he read: “And [due] to Allah from the people is a pilgrimage to the House”(313). A man said: "O Messenger of Allah, every year?" He turned away, then said: "O Messenger of Allah, every year?" He turned away, then said: "O Messenger of Allah, every year?" The Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) said: "By the One in Whose Hand is my soul, if I had said it, it would have become obligatory, and if it had become obligatory, you would not have been able to fulfill it, and if you did not fulfill it, you would have disbelieved. So leave me as I have left you"(314). The anger of the Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) indicates a prohibition against this type of questioning, which leads to an increase in restrictions or an increase in obligations due to excessive clarification and detail. The command for pilgrimage did not specify how many times – in the text – and performing it once suffices for obedience, just as it suffices to perform it twice or three times. If the question necessitates an answer, the answer would be an increase in clarification, which is an increase in obligations that leads to what the Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) informed: “you would not have been able to fulfill it,” thus the lack of specification regarding how many times is a form of concession.
If we consider concession on this basis of understanding, we do not find it to be an area that contains actions without rulings. Rather, it is:
Entities that the legislator has not prohibited, thus they remain permissible based on the principle regarding the nature of things.
Actions whose rulings have been defined by the legislator, but he did not restrict them with additional detail or clarification regarding what the questioner might ask, like the examples we mentioned, and similar to His saying: “And kill them wherever you overtake them”(315). His saying: “kill them” is fulfilled by killing, whether the killing is done by poisoning, stabbing with a dagger, throwing a spear, strangling with a rope or by hand, or slaughtering with a knife, or shooting a bullet, or detonating a bomb, or otherwise. The lack of clarification regarding how to kill is a lack of additional clarification. And leaving the clarification here is the concession. If a questioner were to ask the Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him): "Should we kill them in this way or that way?" his question would provoke the anger of the Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him), as happened in the hadith about pilgrimage. In this regard, the Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) said: "Leave me as I have left you."

(313) Al-Imran/ 97.
(314) Sunan al-Nasa'i, Book of Hajj Rituals.
(315) Al-Baqarah/ 191, An-Nisa/ 91.


[Page 125]

Silence regarding the increase of clarification is not present in all rulings; we notice that some rulings, such as prayer and pilgrimage, do not allow for additional clarification, and Allah knows best. If the legislator were to say, for example, "Establish the prayer" without further detail, the matter would remain ambiguous, and the prayer could be performed in any manner. However, the Messenger (peace and blessings be upon him) provided additional clarification and detailed the matter through his hadiths and his statement, "Pray as you have seen me pray"(316). There is no room for further detail and clarification in this matter, and any change or addition to it is considered an innovation (bid'ah) and is subject to his saying: "Whoever performs an action that is not in accordance with our command, it is rejected."

Thus, no incident is devoid of a ruling, and the ruling indicates its type from among the five types, and stating what does not contradict is not correct.

I must also point out that I am not discussing the ruling of an action when the jurist has exerted their utmost effort and could not reach a presumption regarding a ruling for it.

It should also be noted that regardless of what the correct opinion may be in the area or category of exemption, its subject pertains to things and actions that Allah has pardoned, not to interests. Interests are the result of actions. Therefore, the ruling on what falls within the area of exemption is a ruling on a thing or action, not a ruling on the purpose of the action or its result, whether it is called an interest or otherwise. That is, if it is correct to say that it is permissible or that hardship or punishment is lifted from actions that do not contradict the Qur'an, the Sunnah, consensus, or analogy, then this permissibility or lifting of hardship pertains to the actions themselves and not to their intentions or results.

Thus, the area of exemption is not an entry point for the discussion of unregulated interests.

Acting with the Intention of Achieving Public Interest

What has been previously stated regarding the prohibition of using public interest (maslaha) as evidence for ruling on an action does not mean that seeking public interest is not permissible. It is natural for a person to be inclined towards what they perceive as beneficial and to shy away from what causes them harm; however, the actions they undertake in both cases must adhere to the legal rulings.
The legal rulings are five: what is established as obligatory by evidence must be performed regardless of its outcome, what is established as recommended remains recommended even if we see that there is benefit in abandoning it, what is established as disliked remains so even if it appears to us that there is benefit in doing it or harm in leaving it, and what is established as forbidden must be avoided, even if reason suggests that there is benefit in doing it or harm in leaving it. These rulings do not change except if there is evidence from the Sharia that abrogates them, as in certain cases indicated by the Sharia, such as not cutting off the hand of a thief during a famine, consuming carrion in cases of necessity, and uttering a word of disbelief under duress, and similar situations.
As for what is permissible, which is what the Sharia indicates is allowed, it is natural for a person to act in it according to what brings them benefits—as they perceive them—or to satisfy their desires, or according to what wards off harm—as they perceive it—or what alleviates pain or reduces burdens and restrictions.
Engaging in trade is permissible, engaging in agriculture is permissible, and it is permissible for a person to work as a hired laborer or to be an employer; they may work in whatever they see as beneficial for them, such as what yields them more profit, or what provides them with more comfort, or what aligns with their inclinations and tastes, provided they fulfill the rights of Allah in any work they choose.
A person may consume foods and wear clothing that they like according to their interests, desires, tastes, or inclinations, provided they avoid the forbidden, such as pork, carrion, intoxicants in food, and anything that exposes the private parts or silk for men in clothing.


[Page 128]

Similarly, whoever is entrusted with the responsibility of caring for something is obligated to adhere to the rules of necessity as they are. As for what is permissible (mubah), he chooses according to what he sees as more beneficial, suitable, or correct, such as the caliph in managing the affairs of the ummah, the man in caring for his family, and the woman in managing her husband's wealth.
In all of this, the action is permissible, and its evidence is the Sharia, not the interest (maslaha), rather the interest is the goal that the servant intends from his action.
Thus, interest does not make something lawful or unlawful. Human actions are of two types: acts of worship and customary actions. As for acts of worship, they are a pure relationship between the servant and his Lord, in which the person intends to humble himself before the Creator and to obey Him absolutely, and what the servant gains here is the pleasure of Allah, Exalted is He. There is no interest or worldly return involved.
As for customary actions, such as marriage, divorce, buying, partnership, agency, and acquiring furniture and decorated houses, all of these are actions performed by both Muslims and non-Muslims, each intending certain benefits and interests. The Sharia came and organized the process of benefiting and obtaining interest in these matters and actions. If a person has an interest and desire in sexual relations, the Sharia does not prohibit him from that nor does it leave him free; rather, it has set constraints that must be adhered to, as is the case in buying and selling. The fact that something is an interest does not imply permissibility or prohibition, because the text has allowed us to fulfill our interests but has established a system for that. Ownership is an interest that a person naturally seeks. The Sharia has not prohibited ownership but has defined its causes. Ownership cannot be obtained through gambling, deceit, or theft. Ownership can be achieved through hunting, buying, or leasing. Whoever acquires through lawful buying has intended an interest in the sale, and whoever acquires through usury has intended an interest in usury. The interest in both cases is ownership, which is fundamentally permissible. However, the means of buying is permissible while the means of usury is forbidden. The fact that the intended goal is an interest or permissible does not affect the means to that goal, meaning it does not indicate the permissibility of the action intended to achieve that goal. Allah, Exalted is He, said: “And seek by means of what Allah has given you the home of the Hereafter, and do not forget your share of the world, and do good as Allah has done good to you, and desire not corruption in the land. Indeed, Allah does not like the corrupters”(317).

The legal opinion (ra'y shar'i) and the non-legal opinion (ra'y ghayr shar'i)

Our statement that public interest (maslaha) is not a legal proof does not mean that we consider every ruling whose proof is public interest to be un-Islamic, or that the one who adopts it is sinful. Rather, the ruling remains legal for the one who adopts it if they believe that public interest is a legal proof, or if they are following someone who holds that view.

The status of public interest as a proof or not is a matter of disagreement among the scholarly imams. The Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) said: "If a judge strives to make a ruling and is correct, he will receive two rewards, and if he strives and errs, he will receive one reward"(318). Thus, the ruling derived from the proof of public interest is legal for the one who derived it—based on what they have deemed preferable—and for the one who follows them. The ruling remains respected by those who do not accept public interest, even if it is not permissible for them to adopt it because it is not a legal ruling according to their reasoning or according to whom they follow.

It must be noted that the opinion we respect, even while considering it erroneous, is one that is based on correct reasoning and knowledge and piety. However, the opinion that arises from flattery, personal interest, or partisanship is not a considered or respected opinion.

Those who have advocated for public interest in contemporary times, making it a proof for the Sharia and attempting to rely on some imams in a mistaken manner, and who have issued fatwas based on public interest or the pursuit of benefits and the prevention of harms, permitting usury, or extending hands to the apostate systems that oppose Islam and cooperating with them, or participating in governance under a system of disbelief, or permitting work in usurious institutions, or permitting reconciliation with Israel and recognizing Palestine for it, or permitting seeking assistance from disbelievers to fight Muslims, or refraining from changing those who rule by other than what Allah has revealed under the pretext of facing harm, among many others, their statements and fatwas are rejected and not considered in Islam. They are not weak Islamic opinions; rather, they are non-Islamic opinions, and they are not legal for those who...

__________
(318) Narrated by the two Shaykhs.


[Page 130]

It adopts it. These fatwas are not of the kind of correct legal reasoning (ijtihad), but rather legislation from outside of revelation.
The permissibility of usury (riba), for example, cannot be said to be an Islamic ijtihad opinion for which its proponent is excused, because usury has a definitive text that is certain in its authenticity and meaning, firm, not abrogated, and not specified or restricted: "And He has forbidden usury"(319). Likewise, participation in ruling by disbelief has definitive texts with no room for ijtihad. Allah, Exalted is He, said: "And whoever does not judge by what Allah has revealed, then it is they who are the wrongdoers"(320), and He said: "the disbelievers" and He said: "the wicked". So whoever rules or participates in ruling by disbelief is wicked with interpretation, and a disbeliever if we do not interpret for him. And the one who issues a fatwa permitting it based on public interest (maslaha) is fabricating lies against Allah.
Such rulings are not of the kind of permissible disagreement in opinion, but rather they are a form of misleading in religion, and taking legal rulings from outside the religion of Islam (Have you not seen those who claim to believe in what has been revealed to you and what was revealed before you, wanting to refer to Taghut while they were commanded to disbelieve in it, and Satan wants to lead them astray into a far astray) (321).

[The Back Cover]

We have discussed the unregulated interests (masalih mursala) as a source of legislation and have demonstrated with evidence that there is no proof to support it. We have refuted with evidence and argument all that those who advocate for unregulated interests have imagined to be evidence of its validity. The impetus for this research was the widespread belief among some of the later scholars of usul (principles of jurisprudence) that unregulated interests are a legislative source, and that the interest is evidence for the legal ruling. Wherever there is an interest, there is the law of Allah, and the rules built upon that have been made into legal principles, which they are not, as they are nothing but making interest a religion. In fact, some of the later scholars resort to the books of the imams and the predecessors, extracting texts or fatwas and presenting them to us in meanings other than those intended by the imams. Therefore, it is necessary to warn against the ideas of these individuals, who confuse Muslims regarding the rulings of their religion, and make interest a grand title through which they free themselves from the constraints and rulings of the religion. Thus, it was essential to refute their arguments and claims, and clarify the truth in this matter. Consequently, we have elucidated the position of the scholars regarding unregulated interests, and since al-Shatibi and al-Qarafi have endorsed it, it is necessary to clarify the true meaning of what they intended by their endorsement of unregulated interests.
