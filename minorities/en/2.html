Minority Jurisprudence Between Shar‘ī Necessity and Methodological Compromise

At the close of the twentieth century—coinciding with the growing presence of Muslim communities in Western societies—there emerged a pressing call to develop what came to be known as "Fiqh al-Aqalliyyāt" (Minority Jurisprudence): a legal-theoretical endeavor aiming to adapt Islamic rulings to the context of Muslims living in non-Islamic societies. Undoubtedly, the circumstances of Muslim minorities pose distinct legal challenges, ranging from engagement with secular legal systems to complex matters of personal status such as marriage, legal arbitration, dietary laws, financial transactions, and insurance.

Yet the practical implementation of this trend revealed a deep schism in methodological orientation:

On one side, sincere scholars strove to contain concessions and legal dispensations within a strict usulī (principled) framework, without infringing upon the fixed and categorical rulings of the religion.

On the other side, fatwas were issued by individuals who can only be described as pseudo-jurists—those who expanded the meanings of maslaha (public interest), maqāṣid (legal objectives), and “avoiding the greater of two harms” in an unrestrained and unprincipled manner. They wielded these tools to override even the most firmly established legal rulings using the flimsiest of justifications.

This schism widened to the point of rupture. Under the banners of ease and realism, alien ideas took root—ideas that elevated purely rational, subjective interests (devoid of any grounding in revelation) to the level of legislative authority. The desired outcome became the driver of the legal process itself. Thus was born a “jurisprudence” tailored to justify relaxation in categorical rulings (such as those regarding marriage, usury, or legal arbitration) under the pretense of accommodating minority conditions. This was done by reducing the comprehensive system of the Sharia to a reactive framework for managing Western reality—an effort to align Islamic rulings with Western legal norms and integrate Muslim minorities into Western societies, even if that required reshaping the Sharia itself to fit this purpose.
