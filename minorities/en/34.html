Fourth: Refuting the Political Misuse of the Principle

Invoking the Principle to Justify Participation in Disbelieving Governance:

Some preachers today justify parliamentary elections, supporting secular governments or parties, or endorsing legislation contrary to Sharia by claiming to "prevent a greater harm."

Refutation: This is invalid for two reasons:

Condition of Compulsion is Not Met: A Muslim can abstain from voting or participating in disbelieving governance, and abstention is legally possible.

It is Forbidden to Delegate Legislative Authority to Anyone Who Rules by Other Than God's Revelation: Parliamentary representation involves authorizing the legislator to enact laws—if this is granted religious legitimacy, it constitutes shirk (associating others with God in His legislative right).

3. The Problem of Legislative Delegation (Authorization)

Juristic Argument:

Voting is an explicit authorization for the representative to legislate on behalf of the voter. This makes the voter a partner in every law passed by parliament, as stated in the verse: "Or do they have partners who have legislated for them in religion that which Allah has not permitted?" (al-Shura: 21).

Political Analysis:

Parliamentary systems operate on the principle of a general mandate, which gives the elected member authority to vote on all matters during their term (4–5 years), not only those included in their campaign promises.

Real-World Example:

In 2003, 80% of Labour Party MPs in the UK—including Muslim MPs—voted in favor of the Iraq invasion, despite mass Muslim opposition.
