The artificial distinction between "civil" and "religious": this distinction has no basis in the Sharia. The civil contract that permits enjoyment and seclusion is a marriage upon which legal rulings are established. The Sharia does not recognize a "civil" marriage devoid of rulings.

The replication of a specific ruling (the marriage of a Muslim to a non-Muslim woman) in reverse: this is an analogy with a difference, as the ruling in the Qur'an is specific to the Muslim man and not the Muslim woman, and it does not allow for generalization.

Ninth: Coercion to resort to secular courts in marital disputes and the acknowledgment of their absolute authority:

The established legal ruling: the obligation to refer to the law of Allah. Allah, Exalted is He, said: "But no, by your Lord, they will not truly believe until they make you judge in that which they dispute among themselves" (al-Nisā’: 65). The prohibition of referring to the tyrant (taghut). Allah, Exalted is He, said: "Have you not seen those who claim to believe in what has been revealed to you and what was revealed before you? They wish to refer to the tyrant while they have been commanded to disbelieve in it" (al-Nisā’: 60). There is consensus (ijma) on the prohibition of referring to anything other than the law of Allah in matters that contradict an established legal ruling.

The position of "the jurisprudence of minorities" is concessionary: some fatwas and positions implicitly or explicitly encourage the acceptance of the inevitability of resorting to Western courts in all marital disputes (and sometimes inheritance) without making a genuine effort to apply the Sharia rulings through Islamic arbitration or seeking recognition of the system (except in a very narrow scope). The focus is on "benefiting from the system" and "gaining rights" regardless of the source of the ruling.

Justifications based on public interest (maslaha):

The inevitability of the supremacy of positive law in Western countries and the absence of a complete practical alternative.

Protection of the rights of the weaker party (often the woman) that may be lost if they do not resort to the courts.

Achieving stability and integration without creating conflict with the regime.

Critique of systematic concession:

Wasting a fundamental doctrinal principle: which is the obligation to refer to the Law of Allah and to reject referring to the tyrant. Acknowledging the authority of secular courts that adjudicate disputes is an acknowledgment of their supra-legal legitimacy in organizing the lives of Muslims, which contradicts the methodology of loyalty and disavowal.

Pure rational interest: focusing on "gaining material rights" or "worldly stability" while neglecting the greater right, which is adherence to the Law of Allah and not recognizing the legitimacy of anything that contradicts it. The interest here is purely worldly, separating religion from practical life.
