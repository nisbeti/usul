Minority Jurisprudence Between Shar‘ī Necessity and Methodological Compromise
At the close of the twentieth century—coinciding with the growing presence of Muslim communities in Western societies—there emerged a pressing call to develop what came to be known as "Fiqh al-Aqalliyyāt" (Minority Jurisprudence): a legal-theoretical endeavor aiming to adapt Islamic rulings to the context of Muslims living in non-Islamic societies. Undoubtedly, the circumstances of Muslim minorities pose distinct legal challenges, ranging from engagement with secular legal systems to complex matters of personal status such as marriage, legal arbitration, dietary laws, financial transactions, and insurance.
Yet the practical implementation of this trend revealed a deep schism in methodological orientation:
On one side, sincere scholars strove to contain concessions and legal dispensations within a strict usulī (principled) framework, without infringing upon the fixed and categorical rulings of the religion.
On the other side, fatwas were issued by individuals who can only be described as pseudo-jurists—those who expanded the meanings of maslaha (public interest), maqāṣid (legal objectives), and “avoiding the greater of two harms” in an unrestrained and unprincipled manner. They wielded these tools to override even the most firmly established legal rulings using the flimsiest of justifications.
This schism widened to the point of rupture. Under the banners of ease and realism, alien ideas took root—ideas that elevated purely rational, subjective interests (devoid of any grounding in revelation) to the level of legislative authority. The desired outcome became the driver of the legal process itself. Thus was born a “jurisprudence” tailored to justify relaxation in categorical rulings (such as those regarding marriage, usury, or legal arbitration) under the pretense of accommodating minority conditions. This was done by reducing the comprehensive system of the Sharia to a reactive framework for managing Western reality—an effort to align Islamic rulings with Western legal norms and integrate Muslim minorities into Western societies, even if that required reshaping the Sharia itself to fit this purpose.


[Page 3]

Accordingly, this study critically examines the fundamental contradiction between many of the fatwas issued under the umbrella of Minority Jurisprudence and the categorical scriptural evidences. It exposes:
The mechanisms through which methodical compromises are introduced in matters of marriage, financial transactions, and engagement with secular legal systems.
The transformation of maslaha from a well-regulated Shar‘ī principle into a purely rational justification used to abrogate fixed rulings.
The actors, institutions, and model fatwas that embodied this deviation—turning social integration and Western social pressure into de facto abrogators of revelation.
Thus, this study is driven by a central question:
How did "rational interest"—in some expressions of Minority Jurisprudence—become a tool for dismantling established Shar‘ī principles, rather than a framework for applying them within complex realities?
From Usulī Discipline to Methodological Disintegration
One of the defining features of the Islamic discipline of usul al-fiqh (principles of jurisprudence) is its rigor and rootedness in revelation. It is a science governed by structured methodologies of reasoning and inference, built on systematic principles derived through inductive analysis (istiqrā’), analogical reasoning (qiyās), and interpretive rigor, all framed by sacred sources. Over centuries, these foundations were refined and codified to ensure that the process of legal derivation remains disciplined, objective, and firmly anchored in revealed guidance—not in personal whims or unbounded rational speculation.
Usul al-fiqh is not a set of abstract tools—it is a coherent, integrated system of principles, measures, and methods designed to safeguard the integrity of ijtihād (independent legal reasoning). It ensures that the outcomes of legal reasoning align with the overarching structure of revelation, Islamic theology, and legal order. It revolves around immutable parameters, such as grounding all rulings in revelation, forbidding any purely rational legislation, and engaging with scriptural evidence in a methodologically sound way that ties rulings to the specific realities they are meant to govern. These parameters ensure that ijtihād operates in a way that fulfills its purpose. Thus, every school of Islamic law was not merely a collection of scattered fatwas, nor were its principles arbitrary or eclectic. Each school was a structured intellectual tradition with a coherent usulī methodology. Its rulings reflected its interpretive principles and its system for engaging with texts, ordering evidences, and determining legal precedence.
Islamic epistemology—as explained in the book “Epistemology and Methodologies of Thinking”—was established on the foundational principle of epistemic discipline. From the dawn of Islam, reason was mandated by revelation not as a means to undermine or select from texts, but to understand and apply them within strict methodological boundaries. Therefore, one of the most alarming developments in modern legal discourse is the emergence of “Minority Jurisprudence” in some of its expressions, as a blatant example of the collapse of usulī discipline and the abandonment of the very principles that safeguard valid legal reasoning.


[Page 4]

True Islamic jurisprudence—built upon usul al-fiqh—relies on authoritative sources: the Qur’an, the Sunnah, consensus (ijmāʿ), and analogy (qiyās), as well as overarching juristic maxims (qawāʿid kulliyyah) that guide derivation. Within this framework exists the concept of maṣlaḥa mursala (unrestricted public interest), which is only valid under strict conditions:
It must be real, not imaginary.
It must be general, not particular.
It must be consistent with the Sharīʿah—not contradicting any definitive text, consensus, or established principle.
Its validity must be supported by detailed scriptural evidence.
The primary problem with most applications of “Minority Jurisprudence” is that it substitutes this disciplined Shar‘ī concept of public interest with a purely rationalistic and circumstantial construct—one that is drawn from Western social realities and pressures, with no binding link to scriptural sources or to the established methodologies of derivation in usul al-fiqh. The result has been deeply troubling methodological concessions.
Manifestations of Methodological Breakdown in Minority Jurisprudence
Abandonment of the Conditions for Shar‘ī Maslaha
Scholars such as al-Ghazālī and al-Shāṭibī delineated strict conditions for recognizing public interest as a legitimate basis for legal rulings: it must be real, general, scripturally validated, and not in conflict with definitive texts. They warned against basing legislation on mere whims. In contrast, some applications of Minority Jurisprudence disregard these conditions and elevate pragmatic, worldly considerations—unsupported by detailed evidence—to the level of legislative principles. These are often shaped by temporary circumstances or political and social pressures.
Fragmentary Borrowing from Madhhabs Without Respect for Foundational Methodologies
As emphasized in The Miracle of Islamic Legislation, the danger of picking and choosing between legal schools lies in the fact that every individual ruling is rooted in an integrated usulī framework. For example, if one adopts Imam al-Shāfiʿī’s view that touching a woman nullifies wudu (ritual ablution), and then adopts Abū Ḥanīfah’s opinion that excessive movement invalidates prayer, one is combining fundamentally incompatible premises. This dismantles the usulī foundation upon which both rulings rest. Each school has a distinct methodology for engaging with evidence, and mixing these leads to methodological collapse and a subjective, desire-driven approach to lawmaking.


[Page 5]

Usulī coherence must always be maintained.
Abandoning Ijtihad for Patchwork and Selective Quoting
Authentic ijtihād does not consist of cherry-picking isolated fatwas from old juristic texts without accounting for the context in which they were issued or the usulī principles underpinning them. Yet some figures issuing fatwas under the banner of Minority Jurisprudence invoke anomalous opinions found in obscure legal texts without possessing the intellectual tools of ijtihād or undertaking a systematic usulī study of the issue in question.
An example: In his memoir “Political Memoirs” (p. 299, Egyptian General Book Organization, 1985), Muḥammad ʿAlī ʿUlūbah (former Egyptian Minister of Justice) recounts that Shaykh al-Marāghī (Grand Shaykh of al-Azhar 1928–1930 and 1935–1945) once told lawmakers, “Bring me the Western law you wish to implement, and I will tell you which fiqh school supports it.”
Historical context: This occurred during the British occupation of Egypt, when lawmakers imported Western codes (especially French civil law).
al-Marāghī’s aim: He sought to reduce dependence on the West by providing nominal Islamic justification for these laws by:
Finding superficial resemblances between them and minority fiqh opinions.
Relying on the maxim: “Legal rulings change with changing times.”
Scholarly critique: Scholars like al-Ṭāhir ibn ʿĀshūr in “Maqāṣid al-Sharīʿa” (p. 367) described this as a “patchwork method” that begins with the secular law and then seeks a fiqh-based rationale for it. Ibn ʿĀshūr stated: “It is not valid to make reality the foundation and then subject the texts to it; rather, the texts are the foundation.”
Using Maqāṣid as a Pretext for Overriding Texts
The concept of maqāṣid (legal objectives) has been distorted from its role as a tool for understanding texts into a license for abandoning them. It is now invoked to justify ignoring categorical rulings, under the guise of serving “interests” or alleviating “hardship,” despite the consensus of usul scholars that maqāṣid may only be invoked in the absence of textual evidence—and never to contradict it.
Deviation in Applying Necessity and Hardship


[Page 6]

Minority Jurisprudence has expanded the concept of ḍarūrah (necessity) beyond recognition. Even a doubtful necessity is now used to justify the permissibility of usury, the uncovering of private parts, or abandoning walāʾ and barāʾ (allegiance and disavowal). This represents a dangerous departure from the stringent criteria established in usul al-fiqh: that necessity must be real, certain, temporary, irremediable by other means, and strictly limited in scope.
Conclusion
The practice of “Minority Jurisprudence”—as undertaken by certain actors—reveals a deep methodological crisis. It entails dismantling the usulī architecture that governs ijtihād and replacing it with circumstantial rationalism that ultimately undermines the immutable foundations of the faith.


[Page 7]

True renewal in fiqh does not require demolishing its principles, but rather reactivating them with awareness—reading contemporary realities through the lens of the revealed texts, not reshaping the texts to conform to reality.
Practical Examples That Demonstrate the Danger of Building Upon Minority Jurisprudence
First Case Study: The Fatwa Permitting Interest-Based Loans to Purchase Homes or Cars
In the 1990s, several prominent fatwa-issuing bodies—such as the European Council for Fatwa and Research (ECFR), Dr. Yusuf al-Qaradawi, and the Fiqh Council of North America (FCNA) in 1999, among others—issued rulings permitting Muslims living in the West to take out interest-bearing loans to purchase homes. The justification was framed around necessity, civil integration, and public interest (maslahah), especially in light of high rental costs and the desire to avoid long-term financial harm. They invoked the concept of “removing severe hardship.”
Official Position (Resolution 15/5, 2001):
"It is permissible for a Muslim residing in the West to take out an interest-based loan to purchase a home if the following conditions are met:
(a) No Shar‘i-compliant alternative is available. (b)
The individual fears moral or religious harm to themselves or their family due to lack of homeownership. (c) The housing need is deemed a necessity, not a luxury." (Source: ECFR Journal, Issue 8, p. 183)
Contradiction with Sharia Evidences:
Qur'an: “Allah has permitted trade and forbidden usury (riba).”

“Necessity permits the forbidden.

A Comprehensive Study on the Juristic Principle of "The Lesser of Two Evils" or "The Lesser of Two Harms" Foundational Introduction: The principle of "the lesser of two evils" or "the lesser of two harms" is among the well-known legal maxims frequently cited by jurists. However, it must be precisely understood within the confines of its strict Sharia-based regulations and not misused as a blanket justification for deviations or political participation in systems of disbelief, as some modern Islamic movements have done. This study explores the origin of the principle, the conditions for its application, the supporting evidence, and examples from foundational juristic texts such as those of al-Ghazali and Izz al-Din ibn Abd al-Salam. It also critiques the contemporary misapplication of the principle and refutes its wrongful use.
First: Definition of the Principle and Its Juristic Scope
The Principle: If a morally responsible person (mukallaf) is compelled to commit one of two prohibitions (i.e., cannot avoid both), then he must choose the one that is less harmful.
Its Origin in the Qur'an:


[Page 32]

"Allah does not burden a soul beyond its capacity" (al-Baqarah: 286)
"So fear Allah as much as you are able" (al-Taghabun: 16)
Core Condition: It must be genuinely impossible to abstain from both prohibited actions, and the mukallaf must inevitably fall into one of them.
Second: Juristic Conditions Governing the Principle
Impossibility of Avoiding Both Harms: It must be impossible for the individual to avoid both forbidden acts.
Determination of the Lesser Harm Must Be Sharia-Based: The evaluation must be grounded in revealed law (Sharia), not based on mere whims or utilitarian reasoning.
The Individual Must Be Powerless to Avoid Both Except Through a Greater Harm: He must be unable to avoid both without incurring a more severe consequence.
Documenting the Conditions for Applying the Principle(1):
First: Jurists' Statements Affirming the Condition
Hanafi School: Ibn Nujaym in al-Ashbah wa al-Naza'ir (p. 96): "When two evils conflict, the greater one is avoided by committing the lesser—if there is no alternative but to commit one."
Al-Zayla'i in Tabyin al-Haqa'iq (6/22) quoting al-Kasani: "The lesser harm is committed in cases of necessity, when both cannot be avoided."
Maliki School: Al-Qarafi in al-Furuq (4/204): "If a person is forced into one of two prohibitions and cannot avoid both, he must commit the one with lesser harm."
Al-Mawwaq in al-Taj wa al-Iklil (8/218): "Committing the lesser of two harms is permissible in necessity, provided both cannot be avoided."
Shafi'i School: Al-Ghazali in al-Mustasfa (1/286): "When two evils conflict and one must be committed, then the lesser is committed."
Al-Suyuti in al-Ashbah wa al-Naza'ir (p. 117): "If two evils occur together and both cannot be avoided, then the lesser is committed."
Hanbali School: Ibn al-Qayyim in I'lam al-Muwaqqi'in (3/14): "The Sharia mandates committing the lesser harm when one of two must be chosen and the greater must be avoided."
Al-Buhuti in Kashshaf al-Qina' (6/298): "If one is compelled to commit one of two evils and cannot avoid both, he commits the lesser."

A Comprehensive Conclusion
The principle of “choosing the lesser of two evils” is not a blanket license to bypass divine rulings, nor is it a legitimate justification to normalize what is categorically prohibited. It is a precise and narrowly applicable principle, bound by strict conditions, and only to be used in genuine cases of necessity—and only after exhausting all other legitimate Sharʿī alternatives. To use it outside its context is to engage in human legislation masquerading as Sharīʿah, opening the door to base desires and blurring the line between ḥalāl and ḥarām.
Imām al-ʿIzz ibn ʿAbd al-Salām said: “A harm is not removed by a greater harm unless it is absolutely unavoidable and its prevention takes precedence.”
Thus, the greater harm lies not in committing a wrong, but in clothing that wrong in legitimacy. Participation does not eliminate the evil—it rather transforms it from an external imposition to an internalized, systemically legitimized choice, and that is the greater calamity.
And God knows best. May peace and blessings be upon our Prophet Muhammad, and upon his family and companions.
Conclusion and Recommendations
Certain applications of “Fiqh al-Aqalliyyāt” (Minority Jurisprudence) have led to dangerous methodological concessions in established rulings, particularly regarding marital law and engagement with secular systems—under the pressures of modern reality and in the name of “public interest.” In practice, maslahah has been distorted from a Sharʿī principle grounded in sound legal methodology into a rationalist utilitarianism, tied to Western norms and pressures, resulting in:
Contradicting definitive textual proofs and scholarly consensus.
Undermining foundational creedal tenets, such as ruling by divine law and loyalty and disavowal (al-walāʾ wa-l-barāʾ).
Weakening moral sensitivity toward categorical prohibitions.
Presenting a distorted image of Islam’s “flexibility”—misconstrued as limitless interpretability.
The real challenge for Fiqh al-Aqalliyyāt is not in compromising legal foundations, but in crafting innovative, Sharʿī-compliant solutions within the framework of definitive textual principles and disciplined legal reasoning (uṣūl al-fiqh), not at their expense. This demands:
Unwavering commitment to Sharʿī constants—no matter the pressure.
Strict regulation of the use of necessity, need, and maslahah—within the bounds of Islamic jurisprudence.
Genuine efforts to develop viable Sharʿī alternatives, such as real Islamic financing solutions not affiliated with the capitalist system, recognized Islamic arbitration tribunals, and effective Islamic marriage initiatives.
A clear distinction between lawful facilitation of Islam’s practice in the West and compromising divine law for comfort.
A courageous internal critique of the methodological deviations within minority jurisprudence institutions.

Fiqh al-Aqalliyyāt will only fulfill its religious and societal purpose if it remains anchored in the certainties of revelation, offering a coherent jurisprudential vision that merges Sharʿī authenticity with practical realism—not one where rationalist utilitarianism erases immutable rulings.
