‘Abd Allah ibn Ka‘b ibn Malik (that they gave the medicine to the Prophet, peace be upon him, during his illness, and when he recovered he said: Why did you do that? They said: We feared, O Messenger of Allah, that you had pleurisy. He said: That is a disease Allah would not strike me with. No one shall remain in the house except that it is given to him, except my uncle). So Maymuna was given it while fasting, because of the oath of the Messenger of Allah, as a punishment for them))

It is mentioned in Fath al-Bari by Ibn Hajar (8/147)

((It is learned from it that one who gives a far-fetched interpretation is not excused for it, and there is something to consider in that as well, because what happened was in opposition to the prohibition)) That is, a far-fetched interpretation may be excused if there is no explicit text of prohibition opposing it; whereas this hadith is specific to the case where there is an explicit prohibition contradicting this interpretation. And Allah knows best.

The point of this hadith is that the Companions first gave reasons for the hadith and for the Prophet’s prohibition, peace and blessings be upon him: they asked, why did he forbid us to give the medicine? So they drew out the motive for the prohibition from themselves and said that the motive or reason was only the sick person’s dislike of medicine, and they did not intend the real prohibition. So they went against the real meaning of the prohibition by overturning it, because of what they believed to be a benefit, namely their wish for the Prophet’s recovery, peace and blessings be upon him. Their interpretation thus became very far-fetched, because they opposed the explicit text with their suppositions. For that reason the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, did not excuse them, and punished them with this painful punishment, general to whoever did it and whoever did not, for leaving the apparent meaning for a matter of supposition and an imagined benefit. So this hadith shows the invalidity of giving reasons altogether, the obligation to act on the apparent meaning of the texts, the invalidity of abandoning the evidence for what people suppose to be a benefit, the invalidity of the search for objectives altogether, and that one who leaves the apparent text for some supposed interpretation is not excused, but is punished as well.
