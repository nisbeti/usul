And if it is not permissible for us to ask Him about any of His rulings and actions, then why is this? The causes would thus be entirely invalidated, and the reasons would be completely nullified, except for what Allah, Exalted is He, has explicitly stated that He did such-and-such for such-and-such a reason.

This too is something that should not be questioned; it is not permissible for anyone to say why this cause was for this ruling and not for another, nor to say why this thing was made a cause while another was not. For whoever engages in this questioning has disobeyed Allah, the Mighty and Majestic, and has deviated in religion, contradicting His saying, Exalted is He. (244)

From this discourse, the very important distinction between these terms becomes clear, and using one in place of another leads to significant confusion. However, it is necessary here to clarify the meaning of these terms according to other scholars so that the difference can be understood and clarified.

First, the meaning of cause: Al-Zarkashi said, "In Sharia, the majority say: it is the apparent, defined description that the evidence of the hearing indicates as being a defining characteristic of the legal ruling." He then commented, "Like the setting of the sun being a defining characteristic for the obligation of prayer... like the setting for prayer and the sighting of the moon in Ramadan for the obligation of fasting, and the threshold for zakat." (245) Thus, the cause here is in the same sense that Ibn Hazm meant.

As for the reason (illah) according to the jurists, they mean by it the apparent, defined description that is appropriate to the meaning of the ruling (246). By description, they mean the sign that indicates the existence of the ruling, such as when they say that measuring is the reason for the prohibition of women; thus, a measured item cannot be sold for another measured item involving women, or their saying that intoxication is the reason for the prohibition of a drink. Whether this sign is one that Allah, Exalted is He, has explicitly stated as a cause, as in the case of intoxication—where Ibn Hazm agrees with them—or not, they then resort to deriving a reason, as in the case of deriving the reason for measuring. Here, Ibn Hazm disagrees with them, as he holds that there is no cause except what Allah has made a cause.

The meaning of reason:

The reason (illah) according to the jurists is the apparent, defined description that is appropriate to the meaning of the ruling. Thus, it is a sign of the ruling according to the definition adopted by the researcher from Ibn Hazm's words. First, if it is found, the ruling exists with it. An example is travel being the reason for shortening prayers; when it exists, shortening is obligatory or recommended. However, it is an apparent sign that is not hidden, and it is also defined, not differing with the differences of individuals, places, and times. They have set conditions for this cause: first, that the establishment of this cause has a purpose from the legislator according to the adopted definition, meaning it must have an intention behind its establishment or a goal or wisdom.
