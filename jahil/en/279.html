Al-Shafi'i held that fighting is disliked except in a case of coercion, in which it is permissible. Therefore, the issue of voting for the lesser harm in cases of necessity falls under this ruling all the more so.

The third position: suspension of judgment. This is the position of Ahmad ibn Hanbal. In Masa'il al-Imam Ahmad, narrated by Abu Dawud, in al-Jami' li-Ulum al-Imam Ahmad: al-Fiqh (8/569):

"The captive is asked to fight on the enemy's side in return."

Abu Dawud said: I said to Ahmad: If an enemy were to descend upon the people of Constantinople, and the king said to the captives, 'Go out and fight, and I will give you such-and-such'?

He said: If he tells them, 'I will set you free,' then there is no harm, in the hope that he will escape.

I said: What if he says, 'I will give you something and treat you well'?

He said: The Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) said, "Whoever fights so that the word of Allah may be supreme" (1). I do not know.

Masa'il Abi Dawud (1590)

Nu'aym ibn Na'im narrated from him: I asked Ahmad about a captive in the hands of the enemy, when an enemy of theirs came and fought alongside them. Should he fight with them?

He said: If he fears for his life, or they tell him, 'If you fight with us, we will set you free,' then he should fight with them.

I said: What if he does not fear for his life, and they have not told him, 'We will set you free'?

He said: I have reservations about it.

Tabaqat al-Hanabila, 2/496–497

It is noteworthy that Imam Ahmad permitted him to fight alongside them if they would set him free, despite the fact that fighting alongside them would generally entail enabling the rule of disbelief, and would also involve shedding Muslim blood. But since there was a benefit for him in securing his release from captivity, and this was also a case of coercion, while fighting and killing an original non-Muslim combatant is in principle permissible, he permitted him to do so.
