A rational person for whom it was possible to learn what was forbidden to him, either by consulting the texts that establish its prohibition or by asking the people of knowledge, is deemed to know the prohibited acts. He may not plead ignorance or claim that he did not know. Hence the jurists say: "In the abode of Islam, an excuse based on ignorance of the rulings is not accepted." A legally accountable person is deemed to know the rulings when it is possible for him to know them, not only when he has actually acquired that knowledge. Accordingly, a text establishing a prohibition is considered known to everyone, even if most people have not encountered it or know anything about it, so long as it was possible for them to learn of it. The Sharia did not require actual knowledge, because that would cause hardship, throw the door wide open to claims of ignorance, and obstruct the implementation of the texts. This is the general rule in Islamic law, and it has no exceptions. If the jurists consider it acceptable to plead ignorance of the rulings for someone who lived in a remote desert region without contact with Muslims, or for someone who had only recently embraced Islam and did not live among Muslims, this is not, in reality, an exception. Rather, it is an application of the original rule, which prevents a person from being held accountable for not knowing that something is forbidden until knowledge becomes accessible to him. Such people had no access to that knowledge and are not deemed to know the rulings of the Sharia. But if someone claiming ignorance grew up among Muslims or people of knowledge, his claim of ignorance is not accepted.] (1).
