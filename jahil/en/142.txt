Al-Juwayni said: They have discussed the distinction between fundamentals and subsidiary matters, and said: "A fundamental is that for which there is definitive evidence, while a subsidiary matter is the opposite." I say: This entails circular reasoning. For if it is said, "Fundamentals are established only by definitive evidence," and then it is said, "A fundamental is that for which there is definitive evidence," this is manifestly circular.
Moreover, many practical rulings—in fact, most of them—are supported by definitive evidence, such as the obligation of purification, prayer, fasting, pilgrimage, and almsgiving; the invalidation of ablution by urination and defecation; and the obligation of ritual bathing after a wet dream. Indeed, most of the Sharia's rulings are supported by definitive evidence, while many issues that they classify as fundamentals are supported by probabilistic evidence.
The same is true of the fundamentals of religion and the fundamentals of jurisprudence: there are more examples than can be listed, such as accepting implications, analogical reasoning, their precedence over general texts, the status of a command after a prohibition, the question of whether an era must pass without dissent, the opinion of a Companion, using mursal reports as evidence, the law of those who came before us, and many times as much besides.
Likewise, among the fundamentals of religion are the question of the state, the Lord's subsistence and pre-eternity—whether these are subsistence and pre-eternity additional to His essence—the question of whether necessary existence is intrinsic to the quiddity or additional to it, affirming an attribute subsisting in the self, and other matters. According to this distinction, these and similar issues would be subsidiary, while those practical rulings would be fundamental.
He said: And it has been said: "A fundamental is that in which worship may be performed only in accordance with one specific command, while a subsidiary matter is the opposite."
