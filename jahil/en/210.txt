Once we know that shirk in the language of the Sharia means disbelief, and disbelief means shirk, the verse then clearly indicates that Allah, Mighty and Majestic, does not forgive shirk in any of its forms—unless it is practical shirk (minor shirk), not shirk of the heart. Thus, all the hadiths we cited as evidence that one who commits an act of disbelief on the basis of a mistaken interpretation may be excused also apply to one who commits shirk on the basis of a mistaken interpretation, so long as he has not been negligent in seeking knowledge. Likewise, Allah's saying, "Then those who disbelieve in their Lord equate others with Him"—that is, they regard others as equal to Him; this is shirk, for he has taken his own desire as his god.
The hadith of the white and black thread, as related in Sahih al-Bukhari (6/26):
Musa ibn Ismail narrated to us: Abu Awana narrated to us, from Husayn, from al-Sha'bi, from Adi, who said: Adi took a white cord and a black cord. When it was part of the night, he looked, but could not distinguish them. When morning came, he said, "Messenger of Allah, I put two cords beneath my pillow." He said, "Your pillow must be very wide indeed, if the white thread and the black thread were beneath it."
Here, Adi ibn Hatim interpreted the verse incorrectly and thought that Allah had prescribed this ruling in this manner: that one should begin fasting when the two cords' white and black colors became distinguishable. This was a matter of belief, and an attribution to Allah of something He certainly did not intend. Nevertheless, the Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) did not declare him a disbeliever; rather, he excused him for following the apparent meaning of the wording in the language.
