He said, "What is that?" They said, "Hypocrisy, hypocrisy." He said, "Do you not testify that there is no god but Allah and that Muhammad is His servant and Messenger?" They said, "Indeed, we do." He said, "That is not hypocrisy." They said, "When we are with you, we are in one state, but when we leave you, the world and our families occupy our thoughts." He said, "If, when you left me, you remained in the state you are in with me, the angels would shake your hands in the streets of the city."

This is a sound hadith.

Here, the Companions thought that they had become unbelievers and fallen into major hypocrisy, as indicated by the Prophet's response to them that this was not hypocrisy, so long as they testified that there is no god but Allah. This would not be a valid interpretation if they had meant minor hypocrisy, since that can coexist with the two testimonies of faith. Perhaps these Companions understood this from the common linguistic meaning of hypocrisy: a person outwardly displaying something contrary to what is within. They therefore thought this was hypocrisy in its legal sense, which takes one out of the religion and leads to ruin. The Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) excused them on account of this interpretation, applying the linguistic meaning to the legal meaning. This hadith shows that one who declares another person an unbeliever on the basis of an interpretation is excused.

Umar declared Hatib an unbeliever on the basis of an interpretation, and Usayd ibn Hudayr declared Sa'd ibn Ubadah an unbeliever, saying, "You are a hypocrite, arguing on behalf of the hypocrites." Likewise, Usama ibn Zayd declared the man he had killed an unbeliever, thinking that the two testimonies of faith would not benefit him so long as he had uttered them under the sword. The hadith is in al-Bukhari. Here, Usama interpreted the two testimonies as not, by themselves, protecting a person's life and property or establishing the ruling of Islam when uttered under threat of arms.
