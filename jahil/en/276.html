I shall now present these views, drawing on a writer named Abd al-Aziz al-Tamimi.

First, that the upper hand belongs to the unbelievers, with the Muslim subordinate to them. Is it permissible for a Muslim to fight alongside them against other unbelievers for the sake of money or the like?

Defining the point of disagreement.

The scholars differed over whether it is permissible to join their army and fight alongside them against other unbelievers for worldly gains, such as seeking money and the like. They held four different views on this matter:

The first view: Fighting alongside them is prohibited; this is the position of Malik.

It is stated in al-Mudawwana (1/518):

"I said: What is your view if a group of Muslims are captives in the land of polytheism, or are merchants, and the ruler of that land seeks their help against a group of polytheists who have opposed him, whether they are from among the people of his realm or from outside it? Do you think they should fight alongside him, or not?

He said: I heard Malik say concerning captives who are in the lands of the polytheists, and whom the king calls upon to help him fight his enemy, and who are brought to the lands of the Muslims?

He said: Malik said: "I do not think they should fight over this, nor is it lawful for them to shed their blood over anything like that. Malik said: People are fought only so that they may be brought from polytheism into Islam. As for fighting the unbelievers to bring them from one form of unbelief to another and shedding their blood for that, this is not something that should be done, and it is not fitting for a Muslim to shed his blood over this."

It appears in "Al-Nawadir wa al-Ziyadat ala ma fi al-Mudawwana min ghayriha min al-Ummahat" (14/551):

"If the polytheists sought their help in fighting an enemy of theirs, they should not assist them, unless some of the people of polytheism attack them and take them captive, and the Muslims fear that this will happen to them. In that case, there is no harm in fighting alongside those among whom they are." Here it is worth noting that the Maliki school held it permissible to fight alongside one unbeliever against another if they feared being taken captive. It is also worth noting that, in the matter of voting for the lesser of two evils, Muslims often resort to it out of fear of capture and imprisonment; this case is therefore included here all the more so.
