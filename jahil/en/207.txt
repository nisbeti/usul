It is shirk, not disbelief. The answer to that is: "How can we accept this hadith when its apparent meaning contradicts what is necessarily known of the religion? For this established fact is explicitly stated in the Noble Quran, when the Lord of the worlds said: {Indeed, Allah does not forgive associating anything with Him, but He forgives anything less than that for whomever He wills} [al-Nisa: 48]. This verse is explicit in its indication; that is, in the terminology of the scholars of legal theory (usul al-fiqh), it is definitive in transmission and definitive in meaning. Its meaning is that Allah, Mighty and Majestic, may forgive any sin, however grave, except associating partners with Allah, Blessed and Exalted is He, for Allah does not forgive it: {Indeed, Allah does not forgive associating anything with Him, but He forgives anything less than that for whomever He wills} [al-Nisa: 48]." Someone might say, by way of reconciling this definitive Quranic text with the hadith: "There are two ways to reconcile them. The first is that the verse says Allah does not forgive shirk, whereas disbelief is not shirk.
In other words, what is mentioned in this hadith is not shirk, but disbelief, because this man believed in Allah, Mighty and Majestic, and his belief in Allah led him to make this unjust bequest: he felt that he deserved this punishment, so, to escape Allah's punishment, Mighty and Majestic is He, he made this bequest. He feared Allah; he did not merely believe in Him, but also feared Him. His fear of Allah led him to make this bequest.
Thus, he believed in Allah and did not associate anyone with Him. The way to reconcile the hadith with the verse is to leave the verse with its apparent meaning: {Indeed, Allah does not forgive associating anything with Him} [al-Nisa: 48]. As for disbelief that is not shirk, one could say that Allah may forgive it, and the evidence for that is this hadith.
