The way out of it, or someone telling a doctor about a pain he is experiencing and seeking its relief, involves reports many times more numerous than this, whose truthfulness the hearer is certain of. How, then, can anyone feel at ease and speak freely, claiming that the report of al-Siddiq, Umar ibn al-Khattab, Uthman ibn Affan, Ali ibn Abi Talib, and Abd Allah ibn Masud (may Allah be pleased with them), when they said, "We heard the Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) say such-and-such," does not yield knowledge at all? Glory be to You! This is a tremendous slander.
We testify by Allah that when these men reported something from the Messenger of Allah, their truthfulness was taken as certain. We testify by Allah that when they reported something from other Companions and Successors, their truthfulness was likewise taken as certain. Indeed, we testify by Allah that Salim, Nafi, Said ibn al-Musayyib, and those like them held this same standing; and Malik, al-Awzai, al-Layth, and those like them did too. Neither we nor anyone who knows these men would entertain any doubt about what Malik says: "I heard Nafi say, 'I heard Ibn Umar say, "I heard the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) say..."'" We are certain that our opponents are mistaken in this matter.
A group of scholars of legal theory held that when consensus is reached on acting upon a solitary report, it becomes like a mass-transmitted report. Ibn Burhan related this view, but preferred the opinion that it does not become like a mass-transmitted report. Another group held that if one person makes a claim before a group, asserting its truth in their presence, and they remain silent, his report becomes like a mass-transmitted report. A group of Ahmad's followers and others held that anyone who denies what is established by the report of a trustworthy, upright narrator is an unbeliever. This was the position of Ishaq ibn Rahawayh.
