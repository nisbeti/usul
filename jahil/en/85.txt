Complying with his prohibition of that: as for the one who did it himself, this is clear; as for the one who did not, it is because they failed to forbid what he had forbidden them. It may be inferred from this that an implausible interpretation does not excuse the person who adopts it. There is also a question about this, since the one who acted in opposition to the prohibition— Ibn al-Arabi said: "He intended that they should not come on the Day of Resurrection owing him a right, and thereby fall into a grave predicament." It was objected that he could have pardoned them, since he did not take revenge for himself. What seems apparent is that he intended thereby to discipline them so that they would not do it again; thus it was discipline, not retaliation or revenge.)) Ibn Hajar here transmitted the view of those who said that an implausible interpretation is no excuse: they opposed his explicit prohibition against pouring the medicine into the side of his mouth, on the basis of a far-fetched inference, namely, their assumption that he forbade them only because he disliked the medicine. This was an implausible interpretation, so he punished them all. And Allah knows best.
Shaykh al-Islam said, after mentioning the hadith about the man who instructed his sons, at the time of his death, to burn his body: "One who interprets matters on the basis of ijtihad, while earnestly striving to follow the Messenger (peace and blessings be upon him), is more deserving of forgiveness than someone like this." End quote (3/148).
Ibn al-Wazir said: "There is no disagreement that whoever denies something known by necessity to everyone, and conceals himself behind the name of interpretation in a matter that cannot be interpreted—like the heretics in their interpretation of all the Most Beautiful Names, indeed of the entire Quran, the religious laws, and the afterlife, including resurrection, the Day of Judgment, Paradise, and Hell—is a disbeliever." End quote.
