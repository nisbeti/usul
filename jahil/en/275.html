The issue under discussion is more serious than the issue we are examining, because in the matter of fighting, the Muslim exposes himself to death, the loss of a limb, or severe hardship. People fight only so that their rule will prevail and their word will be supreme. There is no doubt that if a Muslim assists a disbeliever in fighting, he thereby helps him consolidate his rule and, consequently, govern by other than what Allah has revealed.

But the Muslim does not, of course, intend that. Rather, he may have interests in doing so, such as financial gain, inflicting a blow on a more wicked enemy, or warding off harm from himself—for example, if he is a prisoner among them and fights alongside them so that they will release him. I am not here seeking to favor one opinion on this issue; I cite it only to show that even regarding the lowest degree of assistance to a disbeliever—such as a man helping one disbeliever against another solely for financial gain—none of the scholars held that this act constitutes disbelief, even though its consequence is to empower a disbelieving ruler and his rule by other than what Allah has revealed. I have not found any scholar who declared him a disbeliever. Rather, some of them permitted him to do so merely in hope of financial gain, despite the fact that in this matter the Muslim faces the risk of exposing himself to danger to a degree not present when voting for the lesser of two harms in order to avert the greater harm. This risk to oneself was the sole reason that led a group of jurists to prohibit assisting them in return for money alone, rather than out of necessity. There is undoubtedly no such risk to oneself in voting for the lesser harm, which makes applying their opinion to the issue of voting mean that there is nothing wrong with it if it is done to secure an interest and ward off harm from the Muslims.
