The believing man would convey it to the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him), and they would swear by Allah that they had not said it—or they would not swear. At other times, their hypocrisy was evident from their lagging behind in prayer and jihad, their finding the payment of zakat burdensome, and the aversion they showed toward many of Allah's rulings. Most of them could be recognized by their manner of speech—until he said: All these hypocrites outwardly professed Islam and swore that they were Muslims, and they had taken their oaths as a shield. Given their condition, the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) did not impose the prescribed punishments on the basis of his own knowledge, a report from a single narrator, revelation alone, or circumstantial evidence and indications, until the grounds for the punishment had been established by legally admissible testimony or confession—up to where he said: He refrained from killing them, despite their being disbelievers, because their disbelief had not been established by legally valid proof. This is further indicated by the fact that he did not call them individually to repent. It is well known that the best course regarding someone whose hypocrisy and heresy have been established is to call him to repent, as one would an apostate: if he repents, then well and good; otherwise, he is killed. We have no report that he called any one of them individually to repent. It is therefore known that disbelief and apostasy were not established against any one of them individually in a manner that required him to be killed as an apostate. For this reason, their outward professions were accepted, and their inner realities were left to Allah. If this was the case with someone whose hypocrisy had become apparent without legally admissible testimony, then what of someone whose hypocrisy had not become apparent? [(1). (A useful point here): Is someone who knows that a man is guilty of disbelief entitled to regard him as a disbeliever—as in the case described above—even if it is not possible to establish his disbelief through the legally prescribed means of proof?
The answer is yes; indeed, he is obliged to judge him a disbeliever, but on two conditions:
First: The person must be qualified to make the judgment himself, or to seek a legal opinion from someone else, so that he can distinguish disbelief from other things and consider any impediments to making the judgment.
