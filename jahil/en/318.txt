The command to kill them supports the view mentioned, along with the previously cited hadith of Ibn Masud: "It is not lawful to kill a Muslim except for one of three reasons," including "one who abandons his religion and separates from the community." Al-Qurtubi says in al-Mufhim: "The example mentioned in the hadith of Abu Sa'id"—that is, the hadith to be cited in the following chapter—"supports the view that they are unbelievers. For its apparent intent is that they left Islam without retaining any part of it, just as an arrow passes through its prey because of its speed and the strength of the archer, without catching on anything from the prey. He alluded to this with his words, 'It passed beyond the dung and the blood.'" The author of al-Shifa' says: "We likewise decisively judge as an unbeliever anyone who makes a statement that leads to declaring the community misguided or the Companions unbelievers." The author of al-Rawda cited this from him in the chapter on apostasy and endorsed it. Most Sunni scholars of legal theory held that the Kharijites are sinners and that the rulings of Islam apply to them, because they profess the two testimonies of faith and persist in observing the pillars of Islam. They were deemed sinners only because they declared Muslims unbelievers, relying on a corrupt interpretation; this led them to deem the blood and property of those who opposed them lawful, and to testify that they were unbelievers and polytheists. Al-Khattabi said: "Muslim scholars unanimously agreed that the Kharijites, despite their misguidance, are a sect among the Muslim sects. They permitted marrying them and eating their slaughtered animals, and held that they are not declared unbelievers so long as they adhere to the foundations of Islam." Iyad said: "This question nearly became more difficult for theologians than any other, to the point that the jurist Abd al-Haqq asked Imam Abu al-Maali about it, and he excused himself, saying that admitting an unbeliever into the faith and expelling a Muslim from it is a grave matter in religion." He said: "Before him, Qadi Abu Bakr al-Baqillani had refrained from giving a ruling, saying, 'The people did not explicitly declare unbelief; rather, they made statements that lead to unbelief.'"
