In this regard, the case is like that of someone who denied the actual descent to the lowest heaven, interpreting the texts and being mistaken. Does merely having an act accompany a belief make him undeserving of excuse? And who is more deserving of excuse: a man who held a mistaken belief based only on interpretation, or a man who held a mistaken belief based on interpretation and, as a result, performed an incorrect act by which he sought nearness to Allah, mistakenly adding to Allah's religion something that is not part of it as an innovation, if he did so deliberately? So they excused someone who committed two innovative acts, in belief and in practice, but did not excuse someone who committed only one act, namely belief alone.

Ibn Taymiyya said: "He said in Mukhtasar al-Fatawa al-Misriyya: 'Dividing issues into foundational issues, denial of which constitutes disbelief, and subsidiary issues, denial of which does not constitute disbelief, has no basis among the Companions, the Successors, or the imams of Islam. Rather, it was adopted from the Mu'tazila and similar imams of innovation, and they are inconsistent. For when they are asked, "What defines the foundations of religion?" and they answer, "Issues of belief," they may be told: People have disagreed over whether Muhammad saw his Lord, whether Uthman or Ali was superior, many meanings of the Quran, and the authentication of some hadiths. These are matters of belief, yet there is no disbelief in them, by agreement of the Muslims.

"The obligation of prayer, zakat, and pilgrimage, and the prohibition of indecencies and wine, are practical matters, yet one who denies them is an unbeliever, by agreement...

"If they say that the foundations are matters that are definitive, they may be told: Many matters of reasoning are not definitive. Whether an issue is definitive or speculative varies from person to person. What is conclusive for one person may not be conclusive for another, as in the case of someone who hears the wording of the text"
