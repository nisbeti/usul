The exoneration of those whom they declared unbelievers was established with definitive knowledge until their death. This suffices, in our view, to deem unbelievers those who declared them unbelievers. This is supported by the hadith, "If a man says to his brother, 'Unbeliever,' then one of the two has incurred it." In Muslim's wording: "Whoever accuses a Muslim of unbelief, or says, 'Enemy of Allah,' it will rebound upon him." He said: It has been established that these people accuse a group of unbelief, people whose faith we know with certainty; therefore, in accordance with the report of the Lawgiver, they must be judged unbelievers. This is similar to what they said about one who prostrates to an idol and the like, even though there is no explicit denial in such an act, after they had defined unbelief as denial. If they argue that consensus has been established on deeming the perpetrator of such an act an unbeliever, we say: These reports concerning these people likewise entail that they are unbelievers, even if they did not believe with certainty that those whom they declared unbelievers were exonerated. Nor does their general belief in Islam and their performance of the obligatory acts save them from being judged unbelievers, just as it does not save one who prostrates to an idol. I say: Among those who inclined toward some of this discussion was al-Tabari in his Tahdhib. After citing the hadiths of this chapter, he said: "In this is a refutation of the view of one who says that no one among the people of the qibla leaves Islam, once its ruling applies to him, except by consciously intending to leave it. This view is invalidated by the hadith's statement: 'They speak the truth, recite the Quran, and pass out of Islam, yet it does not go beyond their throats.' It is well known that they only deemed the blood and property of Muslims lawful because they erred in their interpretation of Quranic verses, understanding them otherwise than intended." He then transmitted with an authentic chain from Ibn Abbas, and mentioned the Kharijites and what they encounter when the Quran is recited. Ibn Abbas said: "They believe in its clear verses and perish over its ambiguous ones." The command to kill them also supports the aforementioned view, along with the previously cited hadith of Ibn Masud: "It is not lawful to kill a Muslim except for one of three reasons," including "one who abandons his religion and separates from the community." Al-Qurtubi said in al-Mufhim: "The analogy mentioned in the hadith of Abu Sa'id supports the view that they are unbelievers"—that is, the hadith to be cited in the following chapter—"for …"
