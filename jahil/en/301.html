Khabbab ibn al-Aratt (may Allah be pleased with him) said: "I was a blacksmith, and I worked for al-As ibn Wa'il. A debt accrued to me with him, so I went to him to demand payment. He said, 'No, by Allah, I will not pay you until you disbelieve in Muhammad.' I said, 'By Allah, no—not even if you die and are then resurrected.' He said, 'Will I really die and then be resurrected?' I said, 'Yes.' He said, 'Then I will have wealth and children there, and I will pay you.' So Allah, Exalted is He, revealed: {Have you seen the one who disbelieved in Our signs and said, "I will surely be given wealth and children"} (Maryam: 77) (7). A qayin is a blacksmith, and the term is used for any craftsman.

Ibn Hajar, may Allah have mercy on him, said in his commentary: His words, "Chapter: May a man hire himself out to a polytheist in enemy territory?" He included in it the hadith of Khabbab—who was a Muslim at the time—about his working for al-As ibn Wa'il, who was a polytheist. This took place in Mecca, which was then enemy territory. The Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) learned of this and approved it. The author did not state the ruling definitively, since it may be that permissibility was restricted to cases of necessity, or that it was permitted before permission was given to fight the polytheists and oppose them, and before the command not to abase oneself as a believer. Al-Muhallab said: "The scholars disliked this, except in cases of necessity, subject to two conditions: first, that his work be something a Muslim is permitted to do; and second, that he not assist them in anything that would bring harm upon the Muslims." Ibn al-Munir said: "The schools of law have settled on the view that craftsmen in their workshops may work for the People of the Covenant, and this is not considered"
